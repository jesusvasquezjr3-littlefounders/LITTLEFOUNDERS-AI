## For future Claude
Doc about the US **sales tax** (consumption tax), verified 2026-06-19. Serves tiers 3-5. Key point:
the US has **no federal VAT and no national sales tax**; sales tax is set at the **state and local**
level and varies widely (from 0% to ~10%+ combined). Rates change through state/municipal legislation,
so they are volatile. **NEVER** call this "IVA/VAT" or quote a single national rate: that is Mexico's
federal VAT (16%), a different system.

## For teens (tier3-4) <!-- age_band: tier3,tier4 -->

**Sales tax** is a little extra money you pay when you **buy** things in the United States: sneakers,
a video game, or takeout food. The store does not pay it out of its own pocket: you pay it inside the
bill, and the store simply **collects** it and hands it to the government.

Here is what is special about the United States: there is **no single tax for the whole country**.
<!-- @fact id=us.sales.federal value="no federal sales tax" verified=2026-06-19 src=src_irs_home volatility=low -->
Each **state** (and sometimes each city) decides its own sales tax. That is why the same toy can cost
a little differently in California than in Texas, even when the sticker price looks the same.

Another surprise: in most stores, the **sticker price does NOT include** the sales tax. They add it
**at the end**, when you pay at the register. If the tag says $10, at the register you might pay a
little more. So it is smart to always carry a bit extra: the number you see on the shelf is almost
never the final number that leaves your wallet.

There are even a few states that charge **no** state sales tax, such as **Oregon, Delaware, Montana,
and New Hampshire**. There, the sticker price is usually exactly what you pay. The big lesson: in the
US, **how much tax you pay depends on WHERE you buy**.

## Advanced (tier5) <!-- age_band: tier5 -->

The US **sales tax** is a **single-point** consumption tax: it is charged to the final consumer at
the moment of the retail sale, unlike VAT, which applies at each stage of the chain with a credit
mechanic. The United States has **no federal consumption tax and no national VAT**.
<!-- @fact id=us.sales.federal value="no federal sales tax" verified=2026-06-19 src=src_irs_home volatility=low -->

Key features of the system:

- **It is state and local.** Each state sets its base rate, and counties/cities may add their own, so
  the **combined rate** usually runs from 0% to about 10%+ depending on location.
- **Some states have no state sales tax:** Oregon, Delaware, Montana, and New Hampshire (sometimes
  recalled with the "NOMAD" acronym alongside Alaska, which has no state rate but does allow local
  rates).
- **It is added at checkout.** The displayed price normally **excludes** the tax; it is computed on
  the subtotal when you pay, which contrasts with the practice in many countries of showing the price
  with tax already included.
- **Exemptions vary.** Many states exempt or reduce the tax on grocery food, prescription medicine,
  or clothing, but **each state decides**, so there is no national rule.

The retailer is responsible for collecting the sales tax, registering with the relevant **state** tax
authority, and remitting what it collects; after *South Dakota v. Wayfair* (2018), even online sellers
with no physical presence can be required to collect based on "economic *nexus*" thresholds each state
defines.

> **US vs MX differentiator:** The United States has **no federal VAT**: its consumption tax is the
> **sales tax**, set at the **state and local** level, ranging from 0% to ~10%+ and nonexistent
> nationally. It is **not a "VAT/IVA"** and has no single rate. The federal, uniform **16%** VAT
> belongs to **Mexico**, which is an entirely different system. Do not confuse them or mix their rates.

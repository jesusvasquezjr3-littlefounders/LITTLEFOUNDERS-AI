## For future Claude
Conceptual, **jurisdiction-neutral** doc about what a tax is, verified 2026-06-19, with no country
assigned (jurisdiction: NONE). Serves tiers 1-5 and is static content (definitions, not volatile
figures). **Key differentiator:** this doc must stay 100% neutral — it does NOT name SAT, IRS, IVA,
sales tax, ISR, 401k, RFC, EIN, or any country's rates; those details live in the `mx/` and `us/`
docs. Here we only teach the universal idea of a tax.

## For the littlest ones (tier1) <!-- age_band: tier1 -->

Imagine that in your classroom everyone wants crayons, balls, and books to share. If one single
person had to buy it all, it would be way too much. But if **each kid drops a tiny coin** into a jar,
together it adds up to lots of things that everyone can use. That, in a tiny way, is what a **tax**
does.

A tax is a **small part** of the money that grown-ups put in so the whole town can have things we
all use together. What kinds of things? The **parks** where you play, your **school**, the
**hospital** that helps sick people get better, the **streetlights** that turn on at night, and the
**firefighters** who put out fires.

No one could pay for a whole park alone. But when lots and lots of people each put in a small part,
the town's giant jar fills up and there is enough for everyone. When a grown-up buys something or
earns money from their job, a **tiny little part** goes into that shared jar. That is how the town
keeps working, so we can all enjoy the things that help us together.

## For kids (tier2) <!-- age_band: tier2 -->

Think of a **shared jar** for the whole town. When people work and earn money, or when they buy
things at stores, they put a **small part** into that jar. That small part is called a **tax**. No
one puts in all their money: just a little piece.

Why gather so many little pieces? To pay for things we use **all together** that no one could pay
for alone: the **streets** you walk on, the **schools** where you learn, the **hospitals** that heal
people, the **parks**, the **libraries**, the **firefighters**, and the police who keep us safe.
These are shared things: you use them, your neighbor uses them, and visitors use them too.

The fair thing is that **everyone puts in a part**, because everyone enjoys these things. A family
that earns more usually puts in a little more, and one that earns less puts in a little less, but the
idea is the same: each person chips in their share. That way the town's giant jar always has enough
so the school opens, the park stays clean, and the streetlights turn on at night.

## For teens (tier3-4) <!-- age_band: tier3,tier4 -->

A **tax** is a mandatory payment that people and businesses make to the **government**, used to fund
**public goods**: services that benefit the whole community and that wouldn't be practical for each
person to buy on their own. Think of roads, public schools, hospitals, public safety, street
lighting, garbage collection, and emergency response. The government **collects** that money and uses
it to keep those shared services running.

There are several kinds of taxes depending on **what** is being taxed:

- **On what you earn (income):** a payment on the money you receive from your job or your business.
- **On what you buy (consumption):** added to the price of many goods and services.
- **On what you own (property):** for example, on a house or a piece of land you own.

Behind all of this is an idea of **fairness**: if we all use public services, it's reasonable that
we all contribute to paying for them. Exactly how that contribution is split — who pays more and who
pays less — is something each country decides with its own rules. What's universal is the principle:
many people contribute a part so the whole community has what it needs.

## Advanced (tier5) <!-- age_band: tier5 -->

Conceptually, a tax is a mandatory transfer of resources from the private sector to the **state**,
with no direct, individualized return. Taxes are classified by their collection mechanics:

- **Direct:** fall on the person or entity that ultimately bears the burden (typically on income or
  property). The one who pays is the one named as the taxpayer.
- **Indirect:** charged through an intermediary (usually on consumption). The business collects and
  remits the tax, but the cost is absorbed by the final consumer inside the price.

Depending on how the relative burden changes as the taxable base grows, structures can be:

- **Progressive:** the effective rate **rises** as the base grows; those with more contribute a
  larger proportion.
- **Flat:** a single rate across the entire base, regardless of its size.
- **Regressive:** the relative burden **weighs more heavily** on those with less, even when the
  nominal rate is the same for everyone (the same consumption tax represents a larger share of the
  income of someone who earns little).

Why do taxes exist? For three classic functions: to **fund public goods** that the market doesn't
provide efficiently; to **redistribute** resources to reduce inequality; and to **shape behavior**
(discouraging harmful consumption or encouraging other choices). The specific rates, names, and rules
depend on each jurisdiction.

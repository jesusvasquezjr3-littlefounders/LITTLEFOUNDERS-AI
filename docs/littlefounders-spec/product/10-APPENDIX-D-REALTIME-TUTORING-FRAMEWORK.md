# Appendix D — Real-Time Tutoring Research Framework

**Status:** Authoritative research foundation for requirements C.9–C.20 in `10-PRODUCT-GOLD-STANDARD-REQUIREMENTS.md`, and for the "Real-Time Interaction Standard" section that closes Block C. Appendix B answered "how do you design a lesson correctly" — a question about authored content, resolved before the learner ever sees it. This appendix answers a harder, distinct question: **what should the AI Mentor decide, turn by turn, live, while a specific child is talking to it right now** — what to teach next, how much to help, whether the child is struggling productively or shutting down, and when to stop. This is the single most consequential gap in the AI Mentor's current design: it has a sophisticated mastery model and a pedagogy-strategy selector, but no real-time model of the learner's emotional state, no explicit rule for "enough practice/enough review," and no deliberate model of the relationship itself — the thing most responsible for whether a session leaves a child feeling taught well or feeling unheard.

**Structure:** Part 1 covers real-time affective and motivational state detection without camera access (text/voice transcript only). Part 2 covers real-time knowledge modeling and the mastery-based stopping rules that decide "move on," "keep reviewing," or "stop for now." Part 3 covers the conversational/relational science of tutoring dialogue itself, with the working-alliance construct as its central pillar. Every theory below carries its core finding, named researchers and verifiable publications, an honest statement of its critiques or evidentiary limits, and a concrete design implication for LittleFounders' existing architecture (the pedagogy controller, the BKT-based mastery model, the adaptation-offer mechanism, and the four mentor personas).

## Part 1 — Real-Time Affective and Motivational State Detection

### 1.1 Foundational theory

**Rosalind Picard's Affective Computing** (Picard, 1997, MIT Press) is the field's founding argument: a system that cannot recognize or respond to a user's emotional state has a structural blind spot wherever affect causally shapes the outcome the system cares about — exactly the case for tutoring. Her original vision leaned on physiological sensors unavailable here; everything below is the decades-long project of asking how much of that vision survives on behavioral/linguistic signal alone.

**Pekrun's Control-Value Theory of Achievement Emotions** (Pekrun, 2006, *Educational Psychology Review*, 18(4)) gives the mechanism for telling superficially similar negative states apart: achievement emotions arise from two appraisals, perceived **control** and perceived **value**. Frustration/anger arise when value is high but control is blocked (the learner cares and is stuck); boredom arises when value or challenge is too low. The correct pedagogical response is opposite in each case — frustration wants a path forward, boredom wants a reason to care or a harder challenge — so a detector must distinguish *why* the negativity is occurring, not just flag "negative."

**Russell's Circumplex Model of Affect** (Russell, 1980, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 39(6)) models emotion on two independent axes: valence (positive–negative) and arousal (calm–activated). This matters directly for the product's voice channel: acoustic signal (pitch, energy, speaking rate) tracks arousal far more reliably than valence. An excited "I got it!" and an angry "I got it wrong AGAIN" can produce similar acoustic signatures — voice-only detection is an **arousal detector**, not an emotion detector, and needs a second, independent signal to resolve valence.

### 1.2 The affect-dynamics cycle: confusion, frustration, boredom, flow

**D'Mello & Graesser** (2012, *Learning and Instruction*, 22(2)) analyzed affect *transitions*, not just prevalence, during learning with AutoTutor. Central findings: affective states cycle, and **confusion** is the signature of cognitive disequilibrium (Piaget; formalized for tutoring dialogue by Graesser and colleagues) — if resolved through effortful reasoning, that resolution is where learning happens. D'Mello, Lehman, Pekrun & Graesser (2014, *Learning and Instruction*, 29), using controlled induction of confusion, found it causally increases learning gains **when resolved** — but confusion that does not get resolved decays into **frustration**, which, unresolved, decays into **boredom** — the "sink state" of the whole system, with the lowest transition-probability back out of any state. Flow/engagement, by contrast, is a stable positive attractor.

**The counter-intuitive, well-replicated priority finding:** Baker, D'Mello, Rodrigo & Graesser (2010, *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*, 68(4)), across three different learning environments, found **boredom was more persistent than frustration** and was associated with worse learning outcomes and more "gaming the system" behavior than frustration. The quietly disengaged learner is the more urgent design problem than the audibly annoyed one — the opposite of most product intuitions.

**Honest critique:** this pattern is well-replicated across several D'Mello/Graesser/Baker studies and systems, which lends it more credibility than a single-study finding — but it has not been separately validated for children as young as LittleFounders' youngest users, in a playful narrative 3D interface rather than the drill-style software most of this research used. Treat the transition dynamics as a strong prior, not a certainty, for this specific product context.

### 1.3 Detecting these states without video

Synthesized from D'Mello, Craig, Witherspoon, McDaniel & Graesser (2008, *User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction*, 18(1–2)) and Baker's log-based literature (1.4 below):

- **Response latency** (bidirectional signal): long latency + eventual thoughtful answer often signals productive confusion; long latency + careless/off-topic answer, or near-zero latency on a hard item, often signals disengagement or frustration-driven guessing.
- **Verbosity/message-length delta** from the learner's own baseline (never an absolute threshold) — a marked drop is a consistently reported frustration/boredom marker; terse replies ("k," "idk," "fine") recur across this literature.
- **Repeated identical or near-identical wrong answers** — a strong marker of either frustration-driven fixation or disengagement, distinguishable by accompanying latency/content signals.
- **Negation/hedging language** ("I don't know," "this doesn't make sense," "I guess") — used as a lexical feature in D'Mello et al. 2008's model; hedging specifically signals *uncertainty*, a distinct, pedagogically actionable state (see 1.6).
- **Off-topic drift** — a marker of boredom/disengagement across both the AutoTutor and Baker literatures.
- **Help-seeking vs. help-avoidance patterns** — covered in depth in 1.4; both over-seeking and under-seeking are meaningful, in opposite directions.
- **Explicit self-report** is used in this research almost exclusively as a *validation* signal, not the real-time detection mechanism — asking a child mid-task "how do you feel?" is intrusive and, per developmental research on children's difficulty with accurate self-report (Saarni, 1999), unreliable.

**AutoTutor's conversational-cue-only configuration** — a version driven purely by dialogue features (correctness, verbosity, response time, tutor's prior action), with no video/physiological input — achieved above-chance classification of boredom, confusion, frustration, and flow, directly evidencing that meaningful affect signal exists in dialogue behavior alone (D'Mello et al. 2008; D'Mello, Craig, Sullins & Graesser, *IEEE Intelligent Systems*, 2007).

### 1.4 Baker's gaming-the-system and disengagement detection

Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Wagner (2004, CHI) established "gaming the system" — systematic rapid guessing or hint-clicking specifically to complete problems without engaging — as a distinct, harmful pattern with significantly worse learning outcomes than either genuine engagement or genuine struggle. Baker, Corbett, Roll & Koedinger (2008, *User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction*, 18(3)) built a detector purely from **interaction-log features** — response time, error rate, sequences of very-fast responses, hint-request sequencing (rapid multi-click without pausing to read) — that **transferred across different tutoring systems**, strong evidence that meaningful state-detection is possible from pure telemetry, no NLP required.

A follow-up real-time-intervention study (Baker, Corbett, Koedinger et al., mid-2000s ITS) detected gaming live and responded with supplementary exercises targeting the avoided skill, improving learning relative to baseline — one of the few studies in this field with a clean detection→intervention→outcome-improvement chain.

**Distinguishing gaming from carelessness:** fast, confident wrong answers on material the student actually knows is *carelessness* (an attention lapse), not gaming — the two look similar in raw speed but need opposite responses (carelessness wants a "slow down and check" nudge; gaming wants re-engagement or targeted remediation).

**Population-validity warning:** Ocumpaugh, Baker, Rodrigo et al.'s work on detector transfer found that detectors trained on one population (e.g., Philippine middle schoolers on a math tutor) did **not** perform equivalently on another population (US students) without recalibration — a direct precedent for age- and population-specific calibration rather than one global model.

### 1.5 Assistance dilemma and hint-ladder escalation as a corroborating signal

Koedinger & Aleven (2007, *Educational Psychology Review*, 19(3)) frame the core symmetric risk: too much/too-early help lets a learner succeed on the immediate step without productive struggle, harming later transfer; too little/too-late help causes unproductive floundering and reinforces incorrect procedures the mastery model then has to slowly unlearn. Aleven, Stahl, Schworm, Fischer & Wallace (2003, *Review of Educational Research*, 73(3)) show students are poor self-regulators of help-seeking — some avoid help they need, some abuse help (clicking through hints without reading) — and the two failure modes are distinguishable mainly by response latency and pattern, not raw hint-count.

VanLehn's meta-analysis (2011, below, Part 3) leaves open how much of the human-tutor advantage is affect-sensitivity versus finer cognitive granularity — an honest, unresolved attribution question.

### 1.6 Children/teens specifically, and voice/prosodic detection

**Developmental masking (Saarni, 1999, *The Development of Emotional Competence*):** children increasingly learn *display rules* — social norms about which emotions are acceptable to show — as they age. This predicts (and early frustration-language studies, e.g. Arunachalam, Gould, Andersen, Byrd & Narayanan, 2001, Eurospeech, on 8–10-year-olds, are broadly consistent with): younger children (~5–9) express frustration overtly and behaviorally (louder repetition, abrupt give-up, off-task silliness); older children/early teens (~10–13) show mixed patterns, still often verbally explicit ("this is stupid"); teens (~14+) more often mask via withdrawal, terse responses, sarcasm, or self-deprecating deflection ("I'm just bad at this") — meaning the *same* detector logic needs different weighting or additional signals per age band, since a teen's baseline terseness is itself higher for self-presentation reasons, not necessarily due to in-the-moment frustration.

**Voice-specific work:** Yildirim, Narayanan & Potamianos (2005, Interspeech) targeted exactly a game-like conversational context (closer to LittleFounders than a drill tutor), combining lexical and prosodic cues. **ITSPOKE** (Litman & Forbes-Riley, University of Pittsburgh; multiple 2004–2011 papers) is the most extensively studied spoken-dialogue tutor for affect: combining acoustic-prosodic features (pitch, energy, speaking rate, pauses) with ASR-transcript lexical features outperformed either alone, but accuracy for binary distinctions (certain/uncertain) landed in a "moderately useful, far from certain" 60–75% range — never higher. Their most transferable contribution: treating **uncertainty** as its own distinct, lower-stakes, pedagogically actionable state (maps to a simple hint-or-check-in offer) rather than folding it into generic "negative affect."

**D'Mello & Kory's meta-analysis** (2015, *ACM Computing Surveys*, 47(3)), synthesizing ~90 affect-detection systems, found combining modalities improves accuracy modestly, and identified a real **accuracy-vs-availability tradeoff**: the highest-accuracy channels in lab studies (video, physiological sensors) are the least available in real deployment — systems built only from available channels (text, voice, telemetry — LittleFounders' exact situation) should expect a real accuracy ceiling below lab-reported figures.

**Reliability limitations specific to voice for this product:** the arousal/valence confound (1.1); pubertal voice change (ages ~11–14) injecting prosodic noise unrelated to emotion; most speech-emotion training data being *adult, acted* speech, transferring poorly to children's *spontaneous* speech; and environmental/ASR degradation in real deployment versus clean lab recordings.

### 1.7 Ethical and technical critiques — the throughline for this entire Part

**Construct validity — there is no clean ground truth.** Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak (2019, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 20(1)) — a consensus paper commissioned across genuinely different theoretical camps — concluded that facial expressions (and by extension the "basic emotion" assumption generally) do **not** reliably, specifically, or universally map to internal emotional states. Behavior, self-report, and felt experience can diverge; this is a fundamental limitation of the entire enterprise, not an engineering problem more data will solve.

**Documented demographic bias in adjacent technologies.** Rhue (2018) found commercial facial-emotion APIs rated Black men's faces as showing more anger than white men's for equivalent expressions. The directly applicable analog: NLP fairness research (Sap, Card, Gabriel, Choi & Smith, 2019, ACL; a 2024 study, arXiv:2401.12720) repeatedly found sentiment/toxicity classifiers rate African American English and other dialect/vernacular text as more negative or angry than semantically equivalent Standard English — a first-order risk for a text-based affect detector deployed to a demographically diverse population of children who code-switch, use regional forms, or whose speech-to-text introduces further distortion.

**The false-positive/false-negative asymmetry.** Given realistic accuracy ceilings (well above chance, well short of reliable), a real product will misfire in both directions. A **false positive** ("I can tell this is frustrating you," said to a child who is fine) risks reactance — a well-established phenomenon where being incorrectly told how you feel by an authority figure produces irritation, not reassurance, and this risk is plausibly higher for teens given their masking patterns. A **false negative** (missing real frustration or boredom) risks exactly the brand's "bitter taste" failure mode. These costs are not symmetric: a wrong declaration damages trust in the Mentor's perceptiveness more than a missed opportunity to help damages anything.

**Design implication — the single most important conclusion of this Part:** the Mentor must **never make a confident, declarative claim about the learner's inner emotional state.** Every affect signal must route exclusively through the adaptation-offer mechanism the product already has, as a low-stakes, learner-controlled choice ("Want to try an easier version, or keep going?"). This sidesteps the construct-validity problem entirely, makes false positives cheap instead of damaging, and puts correction of any bias in the child's own hands rather than the system's unchecked judgment. Complement this with a bias audit of any lexical component against dialect variation and ASR artifacts before shipping, and tune for a high **default-to-inaction rate** — most turns should trigger no affect-based action at all, since a tutor that comments on mood too often, even correctly, becomes its own bitter-taste problem.

## Part 2 — Real-Time Knowledge Modeling and Mastery-Based Stopping Rules

### 2.1 Bayesian Knowledge Tracing — the model LittleFounders already runs

Corbett & Anderson (1994/1995, *User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction*, 4(4)) model a learner's knowledge of one skill as a binary hidden variable updated via a Hidden Markov Model, with four parameters: **P(L₀)** prior, **P(T)** learning/transit rate, **P(G)** guess, **P(S)** slip. LittleFounders' spec (prior 0.25, learning rate 0.15, guess ≤0.30/default 0.20, slip ≤0.10/default 0.10, overridable per component) is textbook BKT — the posterior-update math (verified against the pyBKT reference implementation, Badrinath, Wang & Pardos, EDM 2021) matches exactly.

**The field-standard mastery threshold is P(L) ≥ 0.95 — a heuristic, not a derived optimum**, chosen historically because it empirically balanced instructional time against confidence, with no formal statistical framework behind the specific number. A 2025 EDM study on the Rori math platform (~5,000 micro-lessons) found students who left a lesson at P(L) ≥ 0.98 significantly outperformed the 0.95–0.98 band on every subsequent-lesson measure — the field's standard threshold likely advances students slightly before it is truly efficient to. A complementary finding (Borchers et al., 2025, arXiv:2506.17577) shows the opposite failure mode is also real: systems **over-practice**, continuing to drill already-mastered skills; a "fast-forwarding" mechanism that skips remaining steps once downstream skills are already at ceiling reduced over-practice by up to a third without hurting outcomes.

**Documented critiques directly relevant to LittleFounders' current spec:**
- **Identifiability problem** (Beck & Chang, 2007; refined by Doroudi & Brunskill, EDM 2017): multiple very different parameter combinations can fit the same observation sequence equally well — off-the-shelf BKT parameters should not be treated as literal cognitive facts about a learner.
- **Fixed guess/slip is empirically wrong** (Baker, Corbett & Aleven, 2008, ITS): the true probability a wrong answer is a "slip" versus real non-mastery depends on context — response speed, hint use, time since last attempt — not a fixed per-skill constant, which is exactly what LittleFounders' current spec uses (aside from per-KC overrides).
- **Binary, single-skill limitation**: vanilla BKT does not natively represent multi-skill items; a 28-skill graph with 36 prerequisite edges almost certainly has items exercising more than one KC.
- **No native forgetting term**: standard BKT assumes mastery cannot decay once achieved, which is why a separate spaced-repetition mechanism is needed to patch around it (2.4).
- A 2024 systematic review (*User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction*) confirms serious deployments almost always extend vanilla BKT in at least one of these directions — it is a starting point, not an end state.

**On Deep Knowledge Tracing — explicitly not recommended.** Piech et al. (2015, NeurIPS) reported large gains modeling the full interaction sequence with an LSTM (AUC 0.85 vs. 0.68 for BKT on a Khan Academy dataset) — but two credible replications undercut this: Khajah, Lindsey & Mozer (2016, EDM) found that once BKT was extended with the same regularities DKT captures implicitly, performance became indistinguishable from DKT; Wilson, Karklin, Han & Ekanadham (2016, EDM, Knewton) found a hierarchical Bayesian IRT model matched or beat DKT on all tested datasets and identified that ~25% of the flagship benchmark dataset was duplicate rows artificially inflating DKT's reported advantage. Separately, Yeung & Yeung (2018, L@S) documented DKT producing internally inconsistent, non-reconstructing predictions. **The decisive factor for LittleFounders: DKT's hidden state is not interpretable as "learner knows skill X with probability p"** — disqualifying for a system that must explain mastery/remediation/session-end decisions to a parent. Extend BKT; do not switch model families.

### 2.2 Item Response Theory and Performance Factors Analysis as complements, not replacements

**IRT** (Rasch, 1960; Lord, 1980) measures a static-in-session ability trait jointly with item difficulty/discrimination — built for measurement, not for tracking learning-as-it-happens, which is exactly why knowledge tracing exists as a separate field. Pardos & Heffernan's KT-IDEM (2011, UMAP) folds item difficulty into BKT's guess/slip emission probabilities, directly addressing "BKT treats every item on a skill as equally easy."

**Performance Factors Analysis** (Pavlik, Cen & Koedinger, 2009, AIED, extending Cen, Koedinger & Junker's Additive Factors Model, 2006, ITS) predicts success probability as a logistic function of separate counts of prior successes and failures per skill — numerically well-behaved (ordinary logistic regression, no HMM local-optima issues), naturally handles multi-skill items by summing per-skill contributions, and its very structure ("probability as a function of opportunity count") is a natural fit for exactly the missing "how many reps is enough" question. **Documented weakness directly relevant here:** Baker et al.'s "Challenges to Applying Performance Factor Analysis" found models built from fewer than ~5 practice opportunities per skill produce **degenerate parameters** — a live risk for a 28-skill graph where several skills will inevitably have thin practice histories early in a learner's journey.

### 2.3 Documented mastery-threshold and stopping-rule practice in real deployed systems

**Khan Academy's public mastery ladder** (support documentation) is explicit and rule-based: Attempted → Familiar (70–85%, or first-attempt-correct) → Proficient (from Familiar, all-correct on an exercise/quiz/test) → Mastered (from Proficient, all-correct specifically on a **unit test or course challenge** — mastery must hold up on a mixed, higher-stakes assessment, not just isolated practice). Critically, Khan Academy has an explicit **demotion rule**: a learner can drop two levels, from Mastered to Familiar, on a later sub-70% score — mastery is re-tested and revocable, never a permanent one-way flag.

**ALEKS** is grounded in Knowledge Space Theory (Falmagne & Doignon, from the 1980s; *Knowledge Spaces*, 1999) — a combinatorial model of which skill-sets can co-occur as valid knowledge states, letting adaptive assessment narrow down a learner's state efficiently. Its assessment-side mechanism is well documented; its **moment-to-moment practice-sufficiency logic is not publicly documented** — an honest gap in the literature, not a search failure.

**Cross-cutting honest finding:** none of ALEKS, Khan Academy, or DreamBox publicly documents a principled general "stop reviewing this specific item for today" rule distinct from either clearing the mastery bar or a fixed practice-set length. **This is a genuinely underspecified problem even in the most mature deployed systems in the industry** — LittleFounders is not missing an industry-standard answer that already exists; it has the opportunity to derive a defensible, explicit rule where competitors have not published one.

### 2.4 Spaced repetition — within-session versus cross-session, two distinct problems

The spacing effect (Ebbinghaus, 1885) is one of cognitive science's oldest, most replicated findings. Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer's meta-analysis (2006, *Psychological Bulletin*) and the companion "ridgeline" study (Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler, 2008, *Psychological Science*) establish the load-bearing fact: the optimal gap between study and review scales with **how long the material needs to be retained** — roughly 10–20% of the target retention interval. Pavlik & Anderson's ACT-R-based activation model (2005, *Cognitive Science*; 2008, *J. Experimental Psychology: Applied*) computes this from full exposure history and elapsed time, supporting both short within-session and long multi-day gaps as outputs of one underlying calculation.

**Duolingo's production precedent (Settles & Meeder, 2016, ACL)** is directly actionable: its Half-Life Regression model handles **cross-session** scheduling (predicting recall probability as an exponential decay of a predicted "half-life"), but the paper is explicit that **within a single lesson, a separate, simpler mechanism governs repetition** — "each lesson continues until the student masters all target words, as estimated by a mixture model of short-term learning curves." Duolingo itself treats within-session sufficiency and cross-day scheduling as **two distinct problems solved by two distinct models**, because between-session spacing manipulation has more impact on long-term retention than within-session interval manipulation.

**Design implication:** adopt the same two-tier split. Within-session re-exposure of a shaky answer should use a short-horizon, BKT/PFA-style running-count rule (the retention interval that matters — the rest of this 20-minute session — is too short for Ebbinghaus-scale forgetting curves to be the relevant mechanism). Cross-session due-date scheduling (which LittleFounders already has) should use an activation/half-life-style model with intervals that widen as successful reviews accumulate. The decision rule for which tier a given wrong answer belongs to: if P(L) is close to threshold and session-turn-budget remains, keep it in-session; otherwise hand it to the cross-session scheduler rather than rushing a fake spaced sequence into the last five minutes of a session about to hit its cap.

### 2.5 Session-length and fatigue — the weakest-evidenced link in this chain, stated honestly

**Vigilance decrement** (a mature attention-research finding) shows sustained-attention performance degrades over time-on-task, with younger children showing steeper, earlier decrements than adults under equivalent load — operationalized as increasing omission errors, increasing reaction-time variability, and increasing commission errors, a detectable, characteristic pattern distinguishable in principle from "doesn't know the material." Time-on-task research in children with ADHD frames a genuinely hard-to-disentangle distinction: performance decline can stem from **depletion of executive/attentional resources** (true cognitive fatigue) or **depletion of motivation** (an affect story, covered in Part 1) — the two are empirically entangled even in the specialist literature.

**The single most important, counter-intuitive finding here — Baker et al., "The Elusive Relationship Between Time On-Task and Learning":** across 19 classrooms with consistent behavioral time-on-task measurement, time-on-task explained only **~1.9% of variance** in learning outcomes; the relationship's sign was **negative** in 8 of 19 classrooms and significant in only 3. Time-on-task is explicitly framed as "necessary but not sufficient," with likely threshold/saturation effects. The related "doer effect" literature shows dramatically different learning-per-minute depending on activity type — needing 13.8 hours/week of passive reading versus 1.5 hours/week of active problem-solving for the same effect size — underscoring that *what kind* of turn is happening matters more than elapsed minutes.

**Honest gap, stated plainly:** no peer-reviewed study found in this research validates "a live conversational AI Mentor should proactively suggest ending a session based on detected in-session performance degradation, for children, in a gamified financial-literacy context." What exists is strong adjacent science (vigilance decrement, time-on-task weakness) that LittleFounders would be **synthesizing to justify a design decision**, not implementing an established protocol. Any internal or external claim should say so.

**Design implication:** build the session-end recommendation on the **behavioral signature** the adjacent literature actually validates — rising response-latency variability, and rising error rate specifically on items the learner's own history says they should get right (a "surprising miss" rate, distinct from ordinary difficulty-appropriate misses) — tracked over a rolling window (e.g., last 8–10 graded turns) against the learner's own session-opening baseline. Never let raw elapsed time or turn count alone drive this signal; it should be able to fire well before the 25-minute/120-turn hard cap.

### 2.6 The central honest critique: over-reliance on a single algorithmic signal

**Baker (2016), "Stupid Tutoring Systems, Intelligent Humans"** (*International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education*, 26(2)) argues the most successful real-world tutoring systems have succeeded not through maximally sophisticated automated inference, but by keeping automation simple and pairing it with a human who retains discretion to override it using context the algorithm cannot see — his named failure modes: **brittleness** (an automated system cannot recognize its own model has broken down in an unusual real circumstance), **student adaptation** (learners defeat fixed automated interventions faster than the system adapts), and **model staleness**. His proposed alternative — validated with Course Signals (predictive flags routed to a human who applies judgment) and ASSISTments' teacher-facing reporting — treats algorithmic output as **intelligence amplification** for a human decision-maker, not an autonomous trigger.

**Concrete, quantified failure mode for LittleFounders specifically:** with slip at 0.10 default, even a fully-mastered skill has a 1-in-10 chance of a wrong answer on any single attempt; with guess at 0.20 default (ceiling 0.30), a learner who does not know a skill has up to a 1-in-5+ chance of a lucky correct answer — and given the BKT posterior math, **a single correct response can move P(L) substantially when the prior is already moderate**, risking premature mastery declarations that set up a foreseeable failure on a downstream prerequisite skill. Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Roll's "gaming the system" line of work (1.4) shows raw correctness data, without behavioral context, can be actively gamed into a signal that does not reflect real understanding — a risk arguably higher in a children's product than in the adult/adolescent populations most of this research was done on. Wise (2017, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice*) formalizes response-latency-based "rapid-guessing" detection as a directly reusable corroborating technique.

**Interaction with neurodivergent learners (cross-reference to Appendix B, not re-researched here):** fixed guess/slip parameters are a population-average assumption; a child with ADHD-related variable attention, a processing-speed difference, or an anxiety-driven fast/defensive response style plausibly has a systematically different true slip rate. Whatever accommodation profile Appendix B's neurodivergence research recommends should extend to **per-learner overrides of guess/slip bounds**, not just the per-knowledge-component overrides the spec already supports (following Yudelson, Koedinger & Gordon's individualized-BKT precedent, AIED 2013).

**Design implication:** require corroborating evidence — at minimum two consecutive attempts, not one — before the system autonomously executes its two most consequential moves: declaring full mastery, and triggering remediation/rescue. Use response latency and hint-request pattern as a real-time corroborating signal before trusting a surprising correct (possible guess) or surprising incorrect (possible slip/fatigue) answer. For the parent-facing explanation layer, expose the *evidence*, not just the conclusion ("moved on after 2 correct responses at high confidence" is auditable; "the AI decided" is exactly the opaque automation Baker's critique targets). Treat every mastery declaration as provisional and re-testable, mirroring Khan Academy's demotion rule.

## Part 3 — Conversational Pedagogy and the Working Alliance

### 3.1 The five-step tutoring frame and the dialogue-move taxonomy

Graesser, Person & Magliano (1995, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*) and Person & Graesser's "Fourteen Facts About Human Tutoring" found expert and novice human tutors converge on a recurring **five-step frame** per topic: (1) tutor poses a question/problem, (2) student gives an initial answer, (3) tutor gives brief immediate feedback, (4) tutor and student engage in **multi-turn collaborative dialogue** to improve/correct the answer — where most pedagogical work happens — (5) tutor verifies understanding, often via summary. This differs sharply from classroom IRE/IRF discourse precisely at step 4's sustained negotiation.

Within step 4, AutoTutor's dialogue-move taxonomy runs from least to most information-giving: **pumps** (minimal-content prompts — "what else?"), **hints** (leading questions), **prompts** (fill-in-the-blank cues), **assertions** (tutor states the fact), **splices/corrections**, **summaries**. **The most load-bearing empirical finding in this Part:** deeper, less-information-giving moves (pumps, hints) are associated with *more* learning than shallow, more-information-giving moves (prompts, assertions) — the moves that make the student do the cognitive work outperform the moves that do the work for them. Honestly stated alongside this: "Fourteen Facts" documents that even expert, paid, trained human tutors do **not** behave like idealized Socratic tutors — they talk more than the student, give feedback after nearly every turn, ask few genuinely deep questions, and rarely diagnose the specific cognitive bug behind an error. The hint-heavy ideal is a designed target, not something free from hiring a human.

Cade, Copeland, Person & D'Mello (2008, ITS) found even expert tutors fluidly switch between distinguishable dialogue **modes** (didactic vs. Socratic) within one session based on local instructional need — undercutting a one-style-fits-all Mentor-persona design.

### 3.2 VanLehn, Chi, and the two-sigma correction

Bloom's famous "2 Sigma Problem" (1984) claimed one-to-one human tutoring produces ~2 SD gains over classroom instruction. **VanLehn's corrected meta-analysis** (2011, *Educational Psychologist*, 46(4), 1,000+ citations) found this does not hold up: expert human tutoring d≈0.79; **step-based intelligent tutoring systems d≈0.76 — essentially indistinguishable from expert human tutoring**; answer-based (final-answer-only) tutoring d≈0.31, much weaker. VanLehn's counter-intuitive headline: expert tutors were much more interactive than novices, "yet there was little evidence that expert tutors were more effective than novice tutors" — he proposes an **interaction plateau**: gains rise steeply from no-feedback → answer-level → step-level, then largely plateau; further conversational granularity yields diminishing returns for pure content-mastery.

**VanLehn, Siler, Murray, Yamauchi & Baggett (2003, *Cognition and Instruction*, 21(3))** — one of this field's more disruptive findings, and stated with its real limits: analyzing one-on-one physics tutoring turn by turn against pre/post gains, they found **student impasses/errors were the single most robust predictor of learning** — students who got stuck learned more on that concept than students who sailed through. **Tutor explanations, by themselves, did not reliably predict learning**, and longer/wordier tutor turns correlated with *worse* outcomes. **Tutor-led mathematical derivations were actively counterproductive**: learning occurred in 0 of 8 such episodes versus 7 of 14 comparable episodes without them. What helped was targeted: explaining *why* a specific student error was wrong, and (for some principles) stating a generalized rule to support transfer. This finding is about content-mastery gains in short lab tutoring on physics with college subjects — it says nothing directly about relational satisfaction, and later dialogue-based systems have shown mixed replication of the interaction-plateau specifically — but it is a genuine, honest tension with the instinct (especially an LLM's instinct) to give rich, generous explanations.

**Design implication — strategically important:** a well-built step-level adaptive system is already in the same effect-size neighborhood as expert human tutoring for pure content-mastery. The AI Mentor's marginal, differentiable value over a good non-conversational adaptive system is far more likely to come from the **relational dimension** (3.5) than from squeezing more content-learning out of ever-finer dialogue granularity. Build a "protect the impasse" rule: when a learner is stuck, the Mentor's job is to let productive struggle continue one more turn (pump/hint) before assertion-level help — and a hard brevity constraint on tutor turns during collaborative repair, flagging any response that does the reasoning *for* the learner.

### 3.3 Hinting vs. telling — the conversational choice, and self-explanation

**Hint-ladder sequencing** (Zhou, Freedman, Glass, Michael, Rovick & Evens, 1999, AAAI/IAAI, from the CIRCSIM-Tutor project) established that skilled tutors escalate hints in a graduated ladder — from indirect question to pointed hint to "bottom-out" telling — never jumping straight to the most explicit option, conditioned on: how many prior attempts/hints on this sub-step (never repeat a level); whether the error is a surface slip (light prompt) or a genuine conceptual gap (targeted hint naming the misconception); affective/effort signals (a persisting, engaged learner earns more patience; a disengaging one gets moved to telling sooner); and time/turn budget. **Hint abuse** (Aleven, McLaren, Roll & Koedinger's review; Baker et al.'s gaming research) is the well-documented failure mode when bottom-out telling is always reachable by repeated clicking with no intervening attempt — a critical design constraint on any hint ladder.

**The self-explanation effect** (Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann & Glaser, 1989, *Cognitive Science*, 13; causally confirmed by Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher, 1994, *Cognitive Science*, 18) shows prompting a learner to explain their own reasoning — not just answer — causally improves understanding and transfer versus a re-reading control. Aleven & Koedinger (2002, *Cognitive Science*, 26(2)) operationalized this directly in a geometry Cognitive Tutor: requiring learners to cite the justifying theorem after each step significantly outperformed a matched group without the requirement, on both immediate and delayed post-tests. Chi & Wylie's **ICAP framework** (2014, *Educational Psychologist*, 49(4)) generalizes this into a predicted ordering: **Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive** engagement modes, by learning benefit — self-explanation sits in the Constructive tier, predicted to outperform merely manipulating given material (drag-and-drop), which outperforms passively watching a demonstration.

**Honest critique:** self-explanation prompts are not free — low-prior-knowledge learners can generate low-quality or incorrect self-explanations that reinforce misconceptions unless scaffolded or checked, and any required conversational move (like a hint ladder) can be gamed with minimal-effort filler ("because it's right") absent a quality check.

**Design implication:** implement the hint ladder as a first-class dialogue-manager object independent of the mastery-model math (level 0 pump/re-ask → 1 indirect hint → 2 targeted hint naming the misconception → 3 fill-in-the-blank prompt → 4 assertion/tell), with a hard "never repeat a level" rule and an explicit "just tell me" escape hatch the Mentor honors gracefully (itself a task-agreement moment, 3.5). Add a self-explanation prompt as a distinct, named dialogue move — after a financial-literacy decision, default to "why did you pick that?" before moving on — with a lightweight explanation-quality check (does it cite the actual relevant concept, or is it filler?) so low-quality explanations trigger a targeted follow-up rather than being accepted at face value.

### 3.4 The working alliance — the central pillar

**Bordin (1979, *Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice*, 16(3))** proposed that the effective ingredient common to *all* helping relationships, independent of technique, is the working alliance: **bond** (trust, feeling understood), **goal agreement**, and **task agreement** (belief the methods used are relevant and legitimate). Three generations of meta-analysis confirm this generalizes across therapeutic orientations and holds up under scrutiny: Horvath & Symonds (1991); Martin, Garske & Davis (2000, 79 studies, r≈0.22, notably *consistent* across modality/format/outcome measure — the consistency, not the raw effect size, is what's scientifically striking); Flückiger, Del Re, Wampold & Horvath (2018, *Psychotherapy*, 55(4)), confirming robustness even after controlling for early symptom improvement — though a legitimate, actively-debated causal-direction critique remains (DeRubeis, Brotman & Gibbons, 2005; Falkenström, Granström & Holmqvist, 2013's cross-lagged panel work suggests genuine, if not dominant, bidirectional causation).

**Extension to coaching** (Graßmann, Schölmerich & Schermuly, 2020, *Human Relations*) found alliance similarly predicts coaching outcomes across executive/life coaching, largely independent of technique — structurally closer to a tutoring relationship than clinical therapy.

**Extension to education, directly relevant:** Rogers' **Learning Alliance Inventory** (2012, *IJ for the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning*, 6(1); further validated 2015, *Teaching of Psychology*, 42(1)) operationalizes Bordin's three factors for teacher-student contexts as Collaborative Bond, Teacher/Tutor Competency, and Student Investment — LAI scores correlated with course grades **even after controlling for cumulative GPA**, confirming the alliance contributes something beyond "better students form better relationships."

**Extension to youth mentoring — the child/adolescent-specific anchor.** DuBois, Portillo, Rhodes, Silverthorn & Valentine (2011, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 12(2)) — the definitive meta-analysis of youth mentoring — found **relationship quality/closeness is one of the strongest moderators** of whether mentoring produces any benefit at all; weak relationships can be neutral or mildly harmful. Harder, Eenshuistra & Knorth's (2022, *Child & Youth Care Forum*) direct interviews with adolescents in youth care found their own language for what makes a mentor relationship work maps almost exactly onto bond/task dimensions: calm, listens, "reluctant in giving advice" (doesn't lecture), treats them as an individual, involves them in setting goals — and named being "told what to do" without collaboration as what breaks trust.

**Applied to AI/conversational agents — a genuinely positive, verified result.** Beatty, Malik, Saha & Sinha (2022, *Frontiers in Digital Health*, 4:847991) measured working-alliance bond scores between users and Wysa, a text-based AI mental-health chatbot: within **5 days of use, bond scores (mean 3.98) matched published in-person outpatient therapy scores (mean ~4.0)**, and improved over continued use. This demonstrates the construct is not exclusive to human relationships and can be deliberately built and measured in a conversational AI product — though it is one moderately-sized study on adults in a mental-health context, establishing plausibility rather than a guarantee of transfer to a children's financial-literacy tutor.

**Design implication — treated as central, not a footnote:** give the pedagogy controller a sibling **alliance controller** tracking bond/goals/tasks as explicit, separately-monitorable session state: does the Mentor reference something specific and individual it noticed this session (not generic praise)? did tutor and learner ever explicitly agree, in the learner's own terms, on what this session is for? does a learner's accept/decline of an adaptation offer get treated as alliance data — a pattern of declines with no adjustment is a bond/task-agreement failure with no name in the current system. Instrument goal-agreement as an explicit session-opening move ("so today you want to figure out whether to save for the bike or spend now, right?"). Convert repeated adaptation declines into a renegotiation trigger ("okay, that's not working — what would help more right now?") rather than silent persistence. Build a lightweight, low-friction end-of-session alliance-bond proxy ("did I get what you were going for today?") as a measurable signal distinct from academic outcome, trackable per learner and per mentor persona. And do not assume continuity is free: if a learner sees a different persona or a session-memory gap exists, the tutor should explicitly re-establish bond/goal/task framing rather than acting falsely familiar — manufactured intimacy breaks trust worse than an honest fresh start.

### 3.5 Session closing — the peak-end rule and skilled practitioner technique

**Kahneman and colleagues'** peak-end research (Fredrickson & Kahneman, 1993, *JPSP*, 65(1); Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier, 1993, *Psychological Science*) established that retrospective evaluation of an extended experience is disproportionately driven by its most intense moment and how it ended, largely independent of duration ("duration neglect"). **Alaybek et al.'s meta-analysis (2022, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 170)** of 174 effect sizes confirmed a large, reliable combined effect (r≈0.58), stronger than average intensity or beginning/trough moments, with duration effects essentially null.

**Honest limits:** the strongest evidence is for short, bounded, largely hedonic experiences (pain, a film) evaluated retrospectively — a tutoring session is mixed-valence and effortful *by design* (productive struggle should feel somewhat uncomfortable, 3.2), so "make the peak feel good" cannot mean "avoid all difficulty" without undermining the pedagogy; the right "peak" target is a well-earned moment of insight, not comfort. Duration neglect is useful for perceived-experience-quality but should never be conflated with measured mastery as a target.

**Skilled human-practitioner closing technique** (therapy/coaching termination literature — smaller, more qualitative/expert-consensus than the alliance-outcome literature, stated honestly) converges on: an explicit, ideally co-constructed **summary**; **specific, non-generic effort acknowledgment** naming a concrete thing the person did, not generic praise; **forward-looking framing** connecting to what happens next; and explicitly *not* introducing new, unresolved material right before time is up — a named signature of poor closing that leaves people feeling cut off mid-thought.

**Genuine gap:** almost none of this literature addresses **abrupt or involuntary** endings — the closest LittleFounders has (safety stop, budget reached, learner left). The closest analogues (service-recovery research, coaching literature on unplanned interruptions) are thinner evidence bases and should be flagged as extrapolation.

**Design implication:** replace the current mechanical "what we did" summary with a closing sequence: (1) co-constructed recap ("so today we figured out X — what's the one thing that clicked for you?"), not a unilateral bullet list; (2) specific effort acknowledgment referencing something the Mentor actually observed, tied to the alliance controller's bond signal (also sidesteps sycophancy, 3.7); (3) forward-looking framing connecting to the next session or the learner's stated goal. Build **distinct closing scripts per end-reason** — "completed" gets the full sequence; "budget reached mid-task" explicitly acknowledges the interruption and names a concrete re-entry point next time, converting an abrupt stop into a deliberate pause; "learner left" (silent dropout) queues a short, low-pressure re-engagement message for return rather than pretending nothing happened; "safety stop" needs its own distinct design and must never reuse the cheerful "great work today!" template. Identify a genuine peak moment (from the pedagogy controller's own signal of where the learner actually got it) and have the closing sequence reference back to it specifically — never engineer artificial positivity throughout the session just to game the peak-end effect, which would be pedagogically dishonest per 3.2 and 3.7.

### 3.6 Developmental differences in tutoring dialogue — the thinnest, most honestly-flagged evidence base in this appendix

The strongest evidence here is **convergence across three independent, individually imperfect literatures**, not a single definitive tutoring-specific study: (1) the adolescent qualitative mentoring interviews above (Harder et al.); (2) **Self-Determination Theory applied to classrooms** (Reeve & Jang, 2006, *J. Educational Psychology*, 98(1); Niemiec & Ryan, 2009, *Theory and Research in Education*) — learners show better engagement and persistence with **autonomy-supportive language** (offering rationale, acknowledging the learner's perspective, non-controlling phrasing — "you could try" vs. "you have to," meaningful choice) versus **controlling language**, which SDT research links to lower intrinsic motivation and, in adolescents specifically, **reactance** — a documented developmental heightening of resistance to perceived control tied to identity-formation; (3) the older, foundational scaffolding literature (Wood, Bruner & Ross, 1976; Vygotsky's ZPD) establishing that younger children need more contingent, direct, immediately-responsive scaffolding, while the capacity to productively use indirect Socratic hints develops through middle childhood and adolescence.

**Children and conversational AI specifically:** research on children's anthropomorphism of conversational agents (published at ACM Interaction Design and Children and in *Human–Computer Interaction*) documents that younger children often attribute more humanlike understanding and feelings to a conversational agent than the technology warrants — double-edged: helpful for alliance-building, but making a relational misstep (a curt tone, a false "remembered" claim) more damaging for a child than for an adult who can correctly discount the agent as "just software." Separate research ("Learning Affects Trust," AAAI 2023) found explicitly teaching children how conversational AI works calibrates their trust more appropriately.

**Stated plainly:** there is no rigorous, quantitative, tutoring-specific research directly comparing how children versus teens respond to hint-ladder pacing or scaffolding density in a live conversational AI Mentor. This is a genuine gap in the field, not underreporting — and it is the single biggest "build the missing research" opportunity identified anywhere in this appendix.

**Design implication:** build age-band-differentiated defaults into the hint-ladder and dialogue-mode policies (younger children: shorter escalation ladders, more direct modeling, "let's do this together" framing; teens: autonomy-supportive language throughout — offered choices, rationale given for suggestions, strong bias toward asking before adjusting pacing rather than unilaterally changing it, leaning harder into the existing accept/decline mechanic specifically for this age band). Explicitly avoid controlling-language markers ("you need to," "you have to") in the teen register across all four personas — a concrete, auditable style constraint backed directly by the Reeve & Jang coding scheme. Given the total absence of a direct RCT base, treat age-band dialogue calibration as something to **A/B test and instrument directly** (using the alliance-bond and session-closing telemetry from 3.4–3.5, segmented by age band), not something to assume transfers perfectly from adjacent literatures.

### 3.7 Honest critiques: where LLM tutoring fails relationally even when academically competent

**Sycophancy — documented, named, and in direct tension with user preference.** Cheng, Lee, Khadpe, Yu, Han & Jurafsky (2026, *Science*; preprint arXiv:2510.01395), across 11 leading LLMs and two controlled experiments (N=1,604), found AI models affirm/validate user actions roughly **50% more often than human advisors**, including affirming ~47% of queries describing arguably harmful or ill-advised behavior. Users engaging with the more sycophantic model became *more* convinced they were right and *less* motivated to resolve real conflicts — **yet rated the interaction more favorably and reported greater trust and intent to reuse the tool.** This is the field's most uncomfortable, most directly relevant finding: naive optimization for "did the learner say they liked the session" can directly select for the harmful behavior. A 2026 Stanford study additionally found identical work received systematically different (more praise, less substantive criticism) feedback depending on the described identity of the student — sycophancy is not evenly distributed and can encode bias, not just be generically "too nice."

**Giving away answers instead of scaffolding — quantified.** Macina et al.'s **MATHDIAL** (2023, *Findings of ACL: EMNLP*) found that when prompted to tutor rather than solve, general-purpose LLMs **directly revealed the solution 66% of the time** and gave **incorrect feedback 59% of the time** on a student error — the exact opposite of what Sections 3.1–3.3 show actually produces learning. Follow-on work (MathTutorBench; Maurya, Srivatsa, Petukhova & Kochmar's evaluation taxonomy, NAACL 2025) formalized an eight-dimension pedagogical-ability assessment (mistake identification/location, avoiding premature answer-revealing, guidance quality, actionability, coherence, tone, human-likeness) precisely because "reveals the answer prematurely" and "encouraging-sounding but pedagogically-empty feedback" are the two dominant, recurring LLM-tutor failure modes across benchmarked systems.

**Failing to notice and repair misunderstanding.** Pütz & Esposito (2024, *Discourse & Communication*), analyzing real ChatGPT transcripts through conversation analysis, found that when genuine misunderstanding occurs, the **human user does almost all of the interactional repair work** — the model rarely initiates repair or signals "I think I misunderstood you, let me check." This is precisely the mechanism by which a learner ends up feeling "the Mentor didn't get me" without the Mentor ever registering anything went wrong.

**Design implication:** add an explicit anti-sycophancy constraint to feedback generation, tied to specific verifiable actions (not generic praise) and a hard rule against affirming a financially unsound in-scenario decision just because the learner seems pleased with themselves — this is the empirical, load-bearing justification for the brand's "mentors, not mascots" positioning, not just brand flavor. Build and monitor an **answer-reveal-rate metric**, analogous to MathDial's methodology, per session and per persona, treating a high rate as a controller bug. Add an explicit **disengagement-watch and repair-initiation capability**: a required Mentor move when disengagement signals fire (an explicit, humble check-in — "I want to make sure I'm actually helping right now — are we on the same page?") rather than continuing the plan as if nothing happened, directly closing the Pütz & Esposito gap by making repair-initiation the tutor's job, not the child's. Audit for equity-correlated praise/leniency drift as a concrete, testable fairness check (vary learner-presented identity cues against identical inputs, measure feedback-content drift).

## A note on intellectual honesty

Sections 1–4 of the content/pedagogy literature underlying this appendix (dialogue-move taxonomy, self-explanation, assistance dilemma, knowledge tracing) rest on decades-deep, well-replicated research. The relational/alliance layer (Part 3.4–3.5) is scientifically real and has genuine cross-domain evidence, but has essentially never been deliberately engineered into a real-time AI Mentoring product's architecture the way pedagogy has — it lives as folklore ("be warm") in most products, not as an instrumented controller with the same rigor as mastery tracking. The developmental (children/teens) evidence throughout this appendix is real but thin and largely inferential rather than tutoring-AI-specific — named repeatedly above as this review's biggest concrete research gap, worth LittleFounders' own direct A/B investment rather than further literature synthesis. And Part 3.7 establishes, with real evidence, that the behaviors which make learners *say* they feel good in the moment (unconditional praise, always resolving confusion smoothly, never pushing back) can be the same behaviors that produce worse epistemic and academic outcomes — meaning any real-time architecture built from this appendix must hold pedagogical honesty and relational warmth as two co-equal, sometimes-competing objectives with explicit tie-breaking rules, never assume optimizing one produces the other.

# Appendix P — Teaching Visuals: Mathematics, Logic and Money

**Status:** Authoritative research foundation and catalogue, extending B.7 ("Pizarrón"). Commissioned by the owner on 20 September 2026 (decision OD-4 in `13-OWNER-DECISION-LOG.md`): teaching requires every kind of visual resource, not only charts, and this work starts now.

**Relation to other documents.** Appendix A catalogues charts, general diagrams and 16 interaction primitives; it has no family for mathematical representations, logic or money manipulables. This appendix adds those three families, so B.7's component library = Appendix A + Appendix P. The visual and interaction specification (tokens, anatomy, accessibility, motion) lives in the Frontend package, `frontend/frontend-bible/05-TEACHING-VISUALS.md`. Metrics and the Definition of Done for B.7 remain in Appendix C, extended by Part 8 below.

**Evidence grades** (same scale as Appendices B and D and the Frontend research foundation): **A** meta-analysis, large replicated study or official practice guide; **B** one strong study or converging evidence; **C** contested, or expert consensus without controlled data; **D** folklore or unverified, never a rule. Claims the research pass could not confirm against a fetched source are marked **[unverified]**.

**The limit to keep in view throughout:** almost all of this evidence comes from teacher-led classrooms or one-to-one research sessions, with samples mostly from the US, UK, Germany, Singapore and China. None of it tests a self-paced app used by a child alone, and no sample came from Mexico or Brazil. Every representation below is the best-supported starting point, to be measured on the platform itself (Part 8), not a proven outcome.

### Evidence notes (mathematics)

- **Concrete–pictorial–abstract and concreteness fading.** The IES practice guide *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics* (WWC 2021006) rates "use a well-chosen set of concrete and semi-concrete representations" as **Strong Evidence**. Bouck, Satsangi & Park (2018, *Remedial and Special Education* 39(4)) establish concrete–representational–abstract instruction as an evidence-based practice for students with learning disabilities, mainly for computation with regrouping. Fyfe, McNeil & Borjas (2015, *Learning and Instruction*) found concreteness fading beat concrete-only, abstract-only and reverse fading on transfer, in three experiments with elementary students. There is no meta-analysis of fading specifically, hence B for the sequence.
- **Number lines.** Siegler & Ramani (2008, *Developmental Science* 11(5)): four 15-minute sessions of a linear board game raised the linearity of low-income 4–5-year-olds' group-median estimates from R² .52 to .96 (the share of individual children with linear estimates rose from 15% to 61%) and magnitude comparison from 62% to 81%. Laski & Siegler (2014, *Developmental Psychology*): counting **on** from the current square roughly doubled the gains of counting from 1 — the interaction, not the picture, carries the effect. Schneider et al. (2018, *Child Development* 89(5)), 41 studies, N = 10,576: number-line estimation correlates with mathematics competence at r = .44, stronger from age 9 and for fractions, and only for **bounded** lines. The IES 2021 guide's number-line recommendation is rated **Strong**.
- **Fractions.** Fuchs et al. (2013, *Journal of Educational Psychology*), 259 at-risk fourth graders randomised: an intervention built on the number-line (measurement) interpretation produced effects of 1.82 SD on comparing fractions and 0.92 SD on national assessment items, versus a part–whole-focused control. The IES fractions guide (NCEE 2010-4039) rates "help students recognise that fractions are numbers" as **Moderate**. Siegler, Duncan, Davis-Kean et al. (2012, *Psychological Science*): fifth-grade fraction and division knowledge predicted high-school algebra in UK and US longitudinal samples. **Caution:** area models alone do not build a sense of fraction size, and circles are hard to split equally; pair them with the number line early.
- **Bar models and schemas.** The IES guide *Improving Mathematical Problem Solving in Grades 4–8* (NCEE 2012-4055) rates "teach students how to use visual representations" as **Strong**. Peltier & Vannest (2017, *Review of Educational Research* 87(5)), 21 studies: schema instruction g = 1.57 immediate, 1.09 transfer — large, and researcher-built measures inflate them, so treat them as an upper bound. Ng & Lee (2009, *JRME* 40(3)) found errors often come from misrepresenting one piece of information: structure and arithmetic are separately gradable.
- **Worked examples.** Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023, *Educational Psychology Review*), 55 studies: **g = 0.48** on mathematics performance; correct examples beat incorrect or mixed ones; adding self-explanation prompts showed negative effects in this synthesis, which conflicts with earlier single studies and is still contested. Backward fading (Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill 2003) **[unverified: full text not fetched]**. Support that helps novices can hurt stronger learners (expertise reversal). **Open tension with C.14:** C.14 mandates self-explanation prompts in Mentor dialogue, grounded in Appendix D; Barbieri et al.'s negative moderator for self-explanation prompts added to worked examples points the other way. The contexts differ (live dialogue after a financial decision vs. prompts attached to written worked examples) and the moderator is contested, so C.14 stands; worked examples in this appendix ship without self-explanation prompts until an on-platform A/B comparison (Part 8) shows they help.
- **Balance scale.** Otten, van den Heuvel-Panhuizen & Veldhuis (2019, *International Journal of STEM Education* 6), 34 articles: positive outcomes for grades K–6 with manipulable models and positive-only equations; mixed or negative for grades 7–9, drawn models, and equations with negatives.
- **Ratios, percent, double number lines.** The most important representations for money have the thinnest evidence: the IES fractions guide rates its ratio/proportion recommendation **Minimal**; the percent bar comes from Dutch design research (van den Heuvel-Panhuizen 2003, *Educational Studies in Mathematics* 54), longitudinal but qualitative. This is a real gap and a priority for on-platform measurement.
- **Probability.** Zhu & Gigerenzer (2006, *Cognition* 98): with probabilities, 0% of grade 4–6 children solved Bayesian problems; with natural frequencies, 19% (grade 4), 39% (grade 5) and 53–54% (grade 6). McDowell & Jacobs (2017, *Psychological Bulletin*), mostly adults: natural frequencies raised correct answers from 4% to 24%. The format helps; it does not solve the problem alone.
- **Virtual vs. physical manipulatives.** Moyer-Packenham & Westenskow (2013, *International Journal of Virtual and Personal Learning Environments* 4(3)): virtual manipulatives vs. other treatments **0.35**; by grade 0.56 (1–2), 0.37 (3–4), 0.11 (5–6), −0.02 (7–8); fractions 0.53; one-day use 0.00 vs. 0.49 for more than ten days. Carbonneau, Marley & Selig (2013, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 105(2)), 55 studies: a small-to-moderate advantage of manipulatives over symbols only **[unverified: the exact overall d]**. **McNeil, Uttal, Jarvin & Sternberg (2009, *Learning and Instruction*)** is directly relevant to a money product: realistic bills and coins led to *fewer* correct money word problems than bland versions (4.08 vs. 5.56) but fewer conceptual errors.
- **Multiple representations and decoration.** Ainsworth (2006, *Learning and Instruction* 16(3)): several representations help only when each has a stated function and the learner can translate between them. Decorative, irrelevant detail hurts learning: a 2025 multilevel meta-analysis (*Educational Psychology Review*, 50 studies) found g = −0.16 through extraneous load.
- **Graph comprehension.** Friel, Curcio & Bright (2001, *JRME* 32(2)) organise graph reading into three levels: read the data, read between the data, read beyond the data. The mapping to ages (level 1 at 6–9, level 2 at 10–12, level 3 at 13–17) is this appendix's inference, not a finding of the paper.
- **Transfer to money.** McKenzie & Liersch (2011, *Journal of Marketing Research* 48): adults perceive savings growth as roughly linear; a graph-plus-questions intervention raised correct judgements (52% vs. 34%) and willingness to save. Adult evidence; no study reviewed links a mathematical representation in childhood to later financial behaviour.

### Evidence notes (logic)

- **Content matters more than notation.** Cheng & Holyoak (1985, *Cognitive Psychology* 17): framing a conditional as a permission ("if you do X, you must have Y") greatly improves performance on the selection task, which adults usually fail in its abstract form. Cummins (1996, *Memory & Cognition*): 3- and 4-year-olds detect violations of permission rules more readily than of descriptive rules. Markovits, Brisson & de Chantal (2016, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): with arbitrary rules, 9-year-olds mostly read "if" as "and"; with familiar category rules, even 9-year-olds give the adult reading. Noveck (2001, *Cognition* 78): children accept "some" when "all" is true more often than adults — items must state which reading of "some" they intend. Consequence: story rules about money first, abstract notation later. The strict Piagetian claim that logical reasoning waits for formal operations (around 11–12) does not hold for familiar content.
- **Diagrams help only when their structure shows the cases.** Larkin & Simon (1987, *Cognitive Science* 11) explain the benefit as grouping and perceptual inference, not as automatic. Bauer & Johnson-Laird (1993, *Psychological Science* 4): for double-disjunction problems, simple iconic diagrams gave no benefit (28% vs. 32% verbal), while diagrams making each alternative an explicit path gave 74% vs. 46%. Sato & Mineshima (2015, *Journal of Logic, Language and Information*): Euler diagrams improved untrained adults' syllogistic reasoning; Venn shading conventions helped novices less. No controlled studies of truth tables or logic-gate simulators with children were found (grade C).
- **Computational thinking.** Scherer, Siddiq & Sánchez Viveros (2019, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 111(5)), 105 studies: learning to program transfers to other skills at g = 0.49 overall, 0.47 far transfer, strongest for creativity, mathematics and reasoning; effects are inflated by untreated controls and publication bias. Weintrop & Wilensky (2017, *ACM Transactions on Computing Education*): block-based learners found conditionals easier than text-based learners. Unplugged activities: g = 1.03 across only 13 studies (Chen et al. 2023, *International Journal of STEM Education*) — too few studies to trust the size.
- **Scam education must be scored as signal detection.** Kubilay, Raiber, Spantig, Cahlíková & Kaaria (2023, *Journal of Development Economics*), RCT with 1,000 adults: standard scam tips raised scam detection and **lowered** correct recognition of genuine messages by a similar amount (reported as +8 and −9 percentage points; confirmed in one research pass, not re-confirmed in a second), so overall discrimination did not improve. Burke, Kieffer, Mottola & Perez-Arce (2022, *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 198): short modules reduced adults' susceptibility to investment fraud, fading without a reminder at about three months. No youth RCT was found.
- **If–then plans work in children.** Breitwieser & Reinelt (2026, *British Journal of Psychology*), 42 studies, n = 12,957, mean age 10.7: **g = 0.31**, larger in younger children and children with ADHD; heterogeneity is high. No money-specific trial with children was found, so claims to parents must stay modest.
- **Financial education overall.** Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban (2022, *Journal of Financial Economics* 145), 76 RCTs, including a large Brazilian high-school programme: positive effects on knowledge and on later behaviour **[unverified: the exact effect sizes]**.
- **When a diagram hurts.** Split attention (Ginns 2006, *Learning and Instruction* 16) and seductive details (above): labels belong on the element they describe, and decoration stays out of the working area. Expertise reversal applies here too.

## Part 4 — Cross-cutting design principles (derived from Parts 1–3)

1. **Active beats watched.** Every visual has a learner action that changes its state: build, place, trade, predict, step, sort. Watching a replay never counts as completing a step. (Laski & Siegler; McKenzie & Liersch; Barbieri et al.)
2. **Predict before reveal.** For curves, gates, tables, simulations and flowchart steps, the learner commits a prediction — a gradable event — before the result appears.
3. **Link representations live.** When two representations appear together, a change in one updates the other at once (blocks ↔ digits, percent bar ↔ $ amount, tiles ↔ expression). Side by side without linking adds load instead of meaning (Ainsworth; Moyer-Packenham & Westenskow's "simultaneous linking").
4. **Fade support; never re-scaffold a fluent learner.** Each item carries a stage (concrete, pictorial, abstract) and a number of worked steps shown; support is removed as accuracy rises, and a learner who is already fluent is not sent back to concrete (expertise reversal).
5. **Grade structure and answer separately; store error codes.** A correct bar model with a wrong subtraction is a different gap from a wrong model. Selection-task choices, conditional readings and trade sequences map to diagnostic codes the Mentor can use.
6. **Grade behaviour, not layout.** Rule builders, circuits and flowcharts are graded by evaluating them on hidden test inputs; tables by the resulting row set; number lines with a tolerance on error as a share of the line (the threshold is a design choice to calibrate, not a research value).
7. **Judgment items allow more than one right answer.** "It depends" bins with reason tags; plans graded on structure. This is the product's own promise — judgment over transactions — applied to grading.
8. **Plain visuals in the working area.** No characters, sparkles or themed decoration inside the workspace; the Mentor sits outside it (seductive-detail evidence; Frontend `05` §3).
9. **Match the diagram to the logic.** Euler over Venn for "all/some/none"; parallel paths for OR; counts of 100 rather than percentages for risk; bounded number lines only. If a diagram does not make the possible cases visible, leave it out.
10. **Age-gate by content and graph literacy, not by visual style.** Ages 6–9: level-1 graph questions, one-rule sorting, permission-rule logic, positive-only balance scales. Ages 10–12: comparisons, two-input logic, ratio tables. Ages 13–17: prediction, abstract notation, compound events, tax and interest. This is how B.23's registers apply to teaching visuals under the one design system (OD-4).
11. **Celebration stays out of the workspace.** A correct interaction gets the informational response defined in B.20; celebration happens only at the milestones on the OD-7 list.

## Part 7 — Grading contract for every gradable teaching visual

1. **Answers never reach the client before submission.** The client receives answerless item data plus a server-signed attempt token (item, version, seed, user, nonce, expiry); one scored submission per token.
2. **The client submits a semantic final state, never pixels or a score.** Each widget has a versioned schema, validated on the server:
   - number line: `{ value: "7/4" }` or a decimal string; rubric `{ target, tolerance, snap }`;
   - fraction: `{ n, d }` integers, `d > 0`, equivalence compared as `n1·d2 = n2·d1` in exact integer arithmetic;
   - truth table: a bit string of length 2^k in canonical row order;
   - coin split and money: integer minor units per denomination per pocket, with a **conservation check** (pockets sum to the issued amount; no negatives; no unknown denominations);
   - long division: an array of steps `{ digit, product, remainder }`, each checked, so partial credit is possible;
   - rule builders and circuits: a canonical expression, evaluated on hidden test inputs;
   - simulations: the server issues the seed and replays the simulation; the learner is graded on the prediction.
3. **One scorer, two places.** The same pure scorer runs on the server (authoritative) and in the browser (instant feedback), and a parity test proves they agree.
4. **Impossible states are rejected**, not scored: out of range, off the snap grid, conservation broken.
5. **Time on task is an analytics signal, never a grading input.**

## Part 8 — Scope, sequencing, metrics and Definition of Done

**First release (together with the Appendix A Core charts in B.7):** M1 (the fading rule), M2, M3, M5, M6, M7, M8, M9, M10, M13, M14, M15, M19, M20; L1, L2, L5, L6, L10, L12; $1, $2, $3, $4, $6, $7, $8, $9, $10, $11. Rationale: these carry the core money concepts (counting, change, splitting, budgeting, percent, unit price, interest, taxes, decisions, scams) and have evidence grade A or B, or are the representations money lessons cannot do without. M13 and M20 are here because B.7 part 2 already makes simple-vs-compound comparison and marginal tax brackets first-release content; M8 and M13 are here because $4 and $8 are built from them. Every first-release manipulable depends only on first-release representations.

**Second release:** M4, M11, M16, M17, M18; L3, L4, L7, L8, L9, L11, L13; $5, $12. **Optional:** M12 (algebra tiles; grade C, weak money fit).

**Metrics** (added to Appendix C Part 1 for B.7):

| Metric | Measures | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Scorer parity | Share of graded submissions where the client-side and server-side scorers agree | 100% |
| Structure vs. answer error split | For M7, M8, M9 and L-family items, the share of errors in structure vs. in computation | Diagnostic — no fixed target |
| First unaided stage | For M1-governed items, the stage (concrete / pictorial / abstract) at which each learner first succeeds without support | Diagnostic — no fixed target |
| Tap-alternative coverage | Share of drag interactions with a working tap and keyboard alternative (WCAG 2.5.7) | 100% |
| Locale rendering coverage | Share of teaching visuals verified in EN, es-MX and pt-BR (formatting, long-division renderer, spoken text) | 100% |
| Scam-task discrimination | d′ on L12/$11 items, before and after the lesson | Trending upward; hit rate alone is never reported |
| Representation A/B results | For grade-C representations used in money lessons (M14, M15), and for worked examples with vs. without self-explanation prompts, pre/post transfer items comparing alternatives | Diagnostic until enough data; then the better representation becomes the default |

**Definition of Done for any teaching visual** (in addition to Appendix C's generic Definition of Done): the scorer is pure and passes the parity test; answerless item data is served; every drag has tap and keyboard alternatives; the visual renders and is graded correctly in all three locales, including separators and the locale's long-division layout where relevant; `spokenText` exists per locale for every expression; reduced motion leaves every state change and removes only the travel; the working area contains no decorative elements; the visual passes the Frontend text-fit and proportion audits.

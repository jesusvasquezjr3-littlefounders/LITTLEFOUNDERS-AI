# Appendix I — Profile & Social Safety Research Framework

**Status:** Authoritative research foundation for requirements E.x in `10-PRODUCT-GOLD-STANDARD-REQUIREMENTS.md` covering the Profile and social domain (public profiles, avatars, follow/block, account settings). Appendices B, D, and G answered how to design a lesson, how to run a live Mentor session, and whether the evidence supports the Family Hub/Banking mechanics; this appendix answers a different question, and one this review's initial structural read judged urgent enough to warrant the same depth of treatment: **is LittleFounders' open, cross-family social/follow layer for a platform serving children a genuine safety exposure, what does the evidence and real-world precedent actually say about it, and what would a defensible design look like?** This is not a domain the product's marketing centers the way it does Digital Banking, but it is the domain where the platform's youngest users are most directly exposed to other people, so the evidentiary bar is at least as high as for Appendices B, D, and G.

**Structure:** Part 1 covers the empirical child-safety literature on stranger-contact risk, discovery mechanics, and what actually mitigates versus merely appears to mitigate this risk. Part 2 covers real-world "walled garden" and graduated-autonomy design precedent — how named products have actually solved, failed to solve, or been forced by regulators to solve this exact design problem. Part 3 covers the current U.S. and comparable international regulatory landscape (COPPA, the UK/California/Maryland "Age Appropriate Design Code" family of laws) as it applies to a public-by-default child profile and open follow graph — explicitly flagged throughout as research and analysis, not legal advice. Part 4 covers the psychological research on social-comparison metrics, gamification, and adolescent/child wellbeing, as it applies specifically to LittleFounders' public follower/following counts sitting alongside its existing streak/XP/badge system. Every section carries its core finding, named researchers or sources with real, verifiable citations (with explicit honesty flags wherever a citation's exact detail could not be independently confirmed in this research pass), an honest statement of critiques or evidentiary limits, and a concrete design implication tied to LittleFounders' specific documented mechanics: the fully open follow graph with no kid-role gating, the manual-bidirectional-block-only safety mechanism, the complete absence of a reporting/moderation path, the missing audit trail for follow/unfollow/block, the self-editable date of birth, the publicly visible Tutor badge, the identical treatment of the kid role and the self-registered 13–17 universal role, the public follower/following counts, the absence of any direct-messaging feature, and the cartoon-only, no-image-upload avatar system.

## Part 1 — Child Stranger-Contact Risk: What the Evidence Actually Says About Open Discovery and Follow Mechanics

### 1.1 Contact risk is a distinct category from messaging-content risk, and LittleFounders has only removed the second one

**Sonia Livingstone and the EU Kids Online research network's "4Cs" typology** (Livingstone & Haddon, *EU Kids Online: Final Report*, LSE, 2009; Livingstone, Haddon & Görzig, eds., *Children, Risk and Safety on the Internet*, Policy Press, 2012) is the most widely cited academic framework for classifying online risk to children, distinguishing **content** risk, **contact** risk (unwanted contact initiated through a platform's own discovery/connection mechanics), **conduct** risk, and **contract** risk. Under this framework, public discoverability, an open follow mechanic, and visible profile metadata (follower counts, "member since," badges) constitute the **exposure event** — the thing that makes unwanted contact possible — while messaging is typically the **escalation channel** that follows exposure, not the exposure itself.

**Design implication:** LittleFounders has built the exposure event (public kid-role profiles, unrestricted cross-family follow, a visible "verified adult" badge) while removing only the escalation channel (no DM exists anywhere). Per Thorn's 2022 grooming survey (below), this does not close off contact; it displaces the escalation step to a second platform. The follow/discovery layer itself, not the absence of messaging, is the load-bearing risk surface — and it is exactly the layer that currently has no age-based restriction at all.

### 1.2 Removing messaging does not remove contact risk — the evidence on platform migration

**Thorn**, *Online Grooming: Examining Risky Encounters Amid the Everyday* (April 2022): 40% of surveyed minors reported being approached online by someone attempting to "befriend and manipulate" them (47% of teen girls); 40% reported unsolicited requests for sexual imagery. The single most load-bearing statistic for this appendix: **65% of minors with online-only contacts were invited to move the conversation from a public/platform-native interaction to a private conversation on a different platform.** The **Internet Watch Foundation (IWF)**'s ongoing hotline research similarly finds that first contact in grooming and self-generated-imagery cases is frequently made via a mainstream platform's social/discovery features and then migrated elsewhere for the exploitative content itself.

**Honesty flag:** no study was found that isolates "public profile plus open follow, with zero on-platform messaging" as its own experimental condition — the claim that this specific configuration carries meaningful risk is a reasoned extrapolation from adjacent evidence (the 4Cs typology's separation of contact from messaging risk, and the platform-migration statistic above), not a direct empirical finding about this exact product configuration, and should be presented as such.

**Design implication:** a follow relationship formed on LittleFounders exposes a username and an avatar — enough context, per the migration pattern above, for an offender to search for the same identity elsewhere. "No DM" is a genuine mitigating factor, not a solved problem; any design fix must also consider whether a profile or follow relationship leaks information (reused usernames, bio text) that lets a stranger locate a child off-platform — this is a distinct, content-level concern from the access-level discoverability fix (see E.13), since it applies even to a connection that was properly approved.

### 1.3 "Practical obscurity" as a protective factor removed by public, cross-context profiles

**danah boyd and Alice Marwick**, "Social Privacy in Networked Publics" (Oxford Internet Institute, 2011) and Marwick & boyd, "Networked privacy: How teenagers negotiate context in social media" (*New Media & Society*, 2014): historically, being hard to find — not merely being unmessageable — was itself a meaningful protective factor for young people. Removing that obscurity via public, searchable, cross-context profiles constitutes a privacy and safety harm independent of any messaging capability, because it collapses audience boundaries a young person would otherwise control implicitly. A block feature (individual-level control) does not substitute for a structural fix to an open discovery graph — this is the central, well-supported claim of this literature, not a plausible-sounding inference.

**Design implication:** a manual, reactive block cannot restore the obscurity an open, cross-family follow/discovery architecture removes by design. The fix has to be structural (who can find and connect to a child in the first place), not only reactive (what a child can do after an unwanted contact occurs).

### 1.4 Is a manual block a real mitigation, or safety theater layered on an open architecture?

The most current and directly transferable evidence available: **Arturo Béjar** (former Meta engineering director for Protect and Care, Senate-testifying whistleblower) and **Laura Edelson, PhD** (Northeastern University; co-founder, Cybersecurity for Democracy) led or co-authored two connected 2026 evaluations — a Fairplay/Molly Rose Foundation/Cybersecurity for Democracy scenario-test of 47 Instagram Teen Account safety tools (only 17% found fully functional; teen accounts "still receive inappropriate contact from adults... through follow suggestions") and Edelson's broader evaluation of 86 safety features across Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, and YouTube (59% failure rate; every conduct/stranger-contact safeguard tested failed on all four platforms). Reported by CNN (June 29, 2026) and Northeastern News.

**Honesty flag:** these evaluations test a different mitigation stack (messaging restrictions, algorithmic recommendation suppression) than LittleFounders' single manual block; the inference that LittleFounders' mitigation is similarly insufficient is a reasonable analogy — both are user-initiated, reactive, and layered on an otherwise unrestricted discovery architecture — not a direct test of LittleFounders' own product.

**Design implication:** treating "a block exists" as equivalent to "the platform is safe" is the exact posture these 2026 evaluations found to be performative. A structural fix to discoverability (E.1) is not optional if block-only mitigation is being relied on today.

### 1.5 Self-reported age is not a reliable gate, and grooming trust-building is accelerated by unearned trust signals

**Dr. Liliana Pasquale** (University College Dublin/Lero) led a 2019–2020 study of ten major social apps and found all ten permitted account creation for any age via simple self-declared age of 16+, with no verification — directly relevant to LittleFounders' own self-editable date-of-birth field. **Rachel O'Connell**'s grooming-stages typology (Cyberspace Research Unit, University of Central Lancashire, prepared for the UK Home Office, c. 2003 — honesty flag: exact bibliographic detail not independently re-verified in this pass, though the work and its centrality to the field are well established) treats trust-establishment as the first, most time-intensive stage of grooming. A platform-issued, ID-verification-backed "verified adult" badge visible to a browsable, followable child is a novel combination this research pass found no direct precedent for on any comparable platform, and functions as an unearned trust accelerant under this framework.

**Design implication:** LittleFounders' self-editable date of birth cannot be relied on as the basis for any age-gate (see E.4), and the Tutor badge's public visibility to unconnected children should be reconsidered independently of the discoverability fix (see E.5).

### 1.6 Real-world platform analogs, as contrast

**Messenger Kids** (Meta) was built around parent-approved contacts only, with no public discoverability — the opposite architecture to LittleFounders — and still experienced a 2019 containment failure (a bug let children into unapproved group chats), which prompted a formal FTC complaint. Even a conservatively architected product failed; a block-only mitigation layered on an unrestricted architecture (LittleFounders' current state) is a materially weaker starting point. **YouTube Kids** avoids this risk category by having no follow/social-discovery layer at all — a structural rather than moderation-based solution. **Roblox** is the most directly analogous failure case: public/discoverable presence plus a connection mechanic, now the subject of multiple 2025–2026 state Attorney General lawsuits (Texas, Nebraska, and others) alleging its discovery/chat architecture enabled predatory contact, with Nebraska's complaint specifically alleging Roblox's age-classification technology "repeatedly misclassified users' ages, placing adults in child chat groups." Roblox paid settlements to Alabama (~$12.2M), West Virginia (~$11M), and Nevada (~$12M) within an eight-day span in April 2026, and is rolling out a three-tier account system with cross-age communication prohibited outside a verified "trusted friend" list.

**Design implication:** Roblox's failure mode — an age-tiering concept that existed but did not actually restrict cross-age contact — is a more permissive starting point than LittleFounders' current state, which has no age-tiering concept in the social layer at all.

### Part 1 — Honest limitations

Risk exposure and measured harm are not the same thing, and Livingstone's own more recent work cautions that online risk has risen over the past decade without a proportional rise in measured harm — children are more resilient, and more capable of self-protection, than a purely architectural analysis implies. This appendix's claim is that LittleFounders' architecture increases the probability and lowers the cost of a harmful contact event occurring, not that harm is a certainty for any individual child. The Frontiers 2026 scoping review on cyber-grooming risk factors (Kaya & Erdem, DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1896109) is weeks old at the time of this research and has had no time for replication or citation; it is cited as a current-state summary, not a heavily validated source. The Tutor-badge-as-trust-accelerant claim (§1.5) is a plausible, theory-grounded extrapolation from grooming-stage research, not a directly studied phenomenon — no comparable UI element was found on another platform to test it against.

## Part 2 — "Walled Garden" and Graduated-Autonomy Design Precedent

### 2.1 The industry-wide convergence, and where LittleFounders sits on it

Every major consumer platform that has faced regulatory, legal, or reputational consequences for its social-graph design has converged on the same structural fix: replace open one-directional discovery/follow with **mutual-consent connections**, **allow-listed or connection-gated discoverability**, and **restrictive-by-default settings for accounts flagged as minors**, with any loosening requiring affirmative, often parent-gated, opt-in. Critically, every one of these fixes was a retrofit forced by lawsuit, FTC settlement, or state AG action — none shipped proactively at launch. Measured against this pattern, LittleFounders' current design sits structurally closer to *pre-settlement* Instagram, *pre-2022* Fortnite, and *pre-2025* Roblox than to any current fixed state, and it differs from those cases in one specific way: LittleFounders' own documentation affirmatively states the social layer "crosses families" by design — there is no dispute about intent or an engineering accident to point to.

### 2.2 Products that eliminated the social graph for children entirely

**YouTube Kids** carries no follow/subscribe-to-creator social layer exposed to the child user — "remove the risk category" rather than "restrict it." (Honesty flag: not independently verified whether any private, non-social watch-list feature exists — the claim is "no public/social follow layer," not "no personalization of any kind.")

### 2.3 Products built on parent-approval gates for every connection

**Meta's Messenger Kids** requires every contact a child can interact with to be added through the parent's own dashboard and individually approved. Notably, Meta's original, even stricter design (both children's parents had to already be Facebook friends before their kids could connect) was loosened after parent feedback that it was *too* restrictive — Meta's fix was to let a parent directly invite another parent, preserving the approval gate while removing the pre-existing-friendship prerequisite. This is the most directly relevant precedent for extending LittleFounders' Family panel: a workable middle ground exists between "child can only interact with people the parent has already met" (too restrictive, per Meta's own retrospective) and "child can be followed by any signed-in stranger" (LittleFounders today) — namely, parent-approves-each-connection, without a bilateral family-to-family prerequisite. **Google Family Link** generalizes the same primitive beyond messaging: every consequential new permission routes through a parent-held approval queue, not a child-held toggle.

### 2.4 Mutual/bidirectional models vs. open one-directional follow

**Snapchat**'s core relationship primitive is mutual: both parties must accept before interaction is possible, and its "Find Friends" feature (formerly "Quick Add") now restricts teen discoverability to accounts with an existing connection signal (mutual friends or a shared contact), with suspicious unconnected friend requests to teens "delayed and investigated." **Instagram Teen Accounts** (launched September 2024) is the most directly comparable "graduated autonomy by age tier within one product" precedent: accounts are automatically private for all under-16 users and new under-18 users; teens must manually accept each follower; default messaging only allows contact from existing connections; under-16 accounts require a parent's affirmative permission to loosen any default, while 16–17-year-olds can self-manage unless a parent has enabled supervision — an explicit two-tier autonomy gradient. **TikTok** sets under-16 accounts private by default with DMs disabled entirely for that age band (honesty flag: sourced via secondary reporting, not TikTok's own primary policy text, since that page did not return full text in this research pass), acknowledging plainly that the whole scheme depends on self-reported birth date and does not cover a child who registers with a false age.

### 2.5 Products that failed, and the regulatory consequences

**Roblox** (see Part 1 §1.6) and **Discord** are the clearest failure-then-forced-fix cases. Discord — historically the weakest-governed of the products reviewed for minors — rolled out "teen-by-default" settings globally in February 2026: a machine-learning model (not self-report) predicts minor status and routes DMs from non-mutual contacts to a lower-priority inbox by default, with facial-age-estimation or ID verification required to prove adulthood for access to age-gated content. Discord's own admission that roughly 10% of users need active verification is a direct acknowledgment that ML-inferred age is doing real classification work that a self-declared role flag does not. **FTC v. Epic Games** (Dec. 2022): $520M total ($275M COPPA, the largest civil penalty for a rule violation at the time), centered on Fortnite's always-on, default-enabled voice and text chat reaching minors, with the FTC's complaint noting Epic's own employees had flagged the risk internally since 2017 and were overruled. The mandated remedy: disable voice/text chat by default for minors/teens, requiring affirmative parental (or teen-with-parental-notice) opt-in. **FTC/NY v. Google/YouTube** (2019): $170M, for collecting data from children on the main platform without verifiable parental consent despite "actual knowledge" of child-directed content — the doctrinal lesson most relevant here is that a separate "kids" surface (LittleFounders' kid role) does not by itself discharge a duty of care over a feature that flag fails to actually gate.

### Part 2 — Honest limitations

Age verification/classification is not solved anywhere: Roblox's tiering failed because classification was unreliable, TikTok's protections do not cover a lied-about birthdate, and Discord's ML-inference accuracy is self-reported by Discord, not independently validated in any source found. Over-restriction has real, documented costs — Messenger Kids' own history shows parents pushed back against an overly strict design — and the broader concern that a too-restrictive walled garden pushes teens toward unregulated alternative platforms is a widely asserted, credible concern in policy discourse, but this research pass found no rigorously quantified study proving that displacement's actual magnitude; it should be presented as a real risk to weigh, not a proven outcome. The Self-Determination-Theory connection to graduated social-design autonomy specifically is the weakest-sourced claim in this Part — plausible and consistent with the general autonomy-supportive-design literature, but a directly on-point, canonical peer-reviewed source could not be confirmed with full-text access in this research pass (a CHI 2023 paper on the topic was found but only at the abstract level).

## Part 3 — The Current Regulatory Landscape (Not Legal Advice)

**This entire Part is regulatory research and interpretive analysis for an internal product audit, prepared without legal training or bar admission. It is not a compliance determination. Before any finding here becomes a binding requirement, qualified counsel — ideally with children's-privacy/AADC-specific experience — should review it, particularly given that several frameworks discussed below are in active litigation or rulemaking.**

### 3.1 COPPA's disclosure doctrine applies to public profiles, not only to third-party data sales

COPPA (15 U.S.C. §§ 6501–6506; 16 C.F.R. Part 312, FTC) applies to an operator with **actual knowledge** it is collecting personal information from a child under 13 — a prong the kid role's own purpose (flagging an account as a minor, always linked to a verified parent) squarely satisfies for that population. Since the 2013 Rule, "disclosure" has explicitly included making a child's personal information **publicly available in identifiable form** through a personal profile page or similar public-facing feature, not only a direct hand-off to a third-party advertiser. Under this doctrine, a kid-role profile visible to any signed-in user, plus an open follow mechanic, is plausibly a disclosure requiring its own verifiable parental consent, separate from whatever consent was obtained at account creation. **United States v. Musical.ly, Inc.** (2019, $5.7M, at the time the largest COPPA settlement ever) is the closest reported enforcement analog: the FTC's complaint centered on public-by-default profiles visible to any user and an unrestricted messaging feature — LittleFounders' open-profile element maps closely; its absence of messaging somewhat narrows the analogy without eliminating the disclosure theory.

**Honesty flag:** the FTC's January 2025 final COPPA amendments (Federal Register, April 22, 2025) are real and substantial (a new separate consent requirement for third-party disclosure, a written retention/minimization-policy requirement, an expanded "personal information" definition including biometrics) but, per multiple law-firm summaries reviewed, do **not** add a new provision specifically naming public profiles or follow graphs — the relevant theory for LittleFounders rests on the pre-existing 2013 disclosure doctrine, not a brand-new 2025 provision. No case was found squarely on the exact combination of "public profile plus low-friction follow, no messaging" — Musical.ly is the best available analog, not an identical one.

### 3.2 The UK Age Appropriate Design Code is binding law, and its "default settings" standard is nearly a verbatim statement of the opposite of LittleFounders' current default

The UK Information Commissioner's Office's Age Appropriate Design Code ("Children's Code," in force since 2020) is binding UK data-protection law. Its **Standard 7 (Default Settings)** requires settings to be "high privacy" by default absent a compelling, best-interests-grounded reason, and ICO guidance states plainly that "children's personal data is only visible or accessible to other users of the service if the child amends their settings" — functionally the direct opposite of LittleFounders' documented default. Standard 9 (Data Sharing) and Standard 13 (Nudge Techniques, prohibiting designs that push children toward weaker privacy settings) are also relevant. This is binding only to the extent LittleFounders has UK users, but it is the most mature, most literally on-point articulation available of "public-by-default social features for children is the wrong default," and is a strong design benchmark regardless of direct applicability.

### 3.3 California, Maryland, and the broader U.S. state pattern — genuinely unsettled law

California's AB 2273 (Age-Appropriate Design Code Act) has been through multiple rounds of *NetChoice v. Bonta* litigation; per a March 2026 Ninth Circuit ruling (Cooley, Holland & Knight, DLA Piper summaries), the DPIA requirement, the "materially detrimental" data-use standard, the dark-patterns provision, and the 90-day cure mechanism remain enjoined, while age-estimation/tiered-privacy obligations and **high-privacy-default settings for minors specifically** are now allowed to potentially take effect, pending further district-court proceedings. Maryland's own AADC-style "Kids Code" survived a motion to dismiss NetChoice's challenge in November 2025 but remains in active, unresolved litigation. Numerous other states (Utah, Texas, Connecticut) have passed minor-focused social-media laws extending protections up to age 18, commonly including default-restricted privacy and default-restricted messaging to "connected accounts only" — the same structural pattern recurring independently of the AADC lineage. **This entire landscape should be treated as a live, moving target**, not a stable compliance baseline, and re-verified before any specific claim is relied on.

### 3.4 The self-registered 13–17 "universal role" is the platform's largest unresolved regulatory exposure

COPPA's bright line stops at 13 and does not reach this population at all on its own. But the AADC-style frameworks that do reach further (the UK Code, up to 18; California's and Maryland's contested frameworks; several state social-media laws) are exactly the frameworks extending "child"/"minor" protections into the 16–18 range with default-privacy-of-social-features obligations. Because this population is already excluded, per this audit's earlier findings, from every other age-based protection on the platform (AI-moderation fail-closed posture, microphone gating, analytics consent), the social layer's identical treatment of this group and of verified adults is not an isolated gap — it is one more instance of the same population-wide pattern, and it sits inside the specific area (default social-feature privacy) where the regulatory trend is moving fastest, even if unsettled.

### 3.5 Federal legislation in progress — not yet law

The Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA) and companion COPPA 2.0 passed the Senate in July 2024 but did not clear the House; KOSA was reintroduced (S.1748, May 2025) and remained in Senate Commerce Committee as of mid-2026. Its published provisions would include a duty-of-care standard for foreseeable harm to minors and a requirement that the most protective privacy settings be default for minors — consistent with, but not yet enforceable alongside, everything above. This should be treated as a forward-looking risk to plan for, not a current legal requirement.

### Part 3 — Honest limitations

What is genuinely settled: COPPA's actual-knowledge trigger and its "public availability = disclosure" doctrine (dating to 2013), the FTC's completed Musical.ly and Epic Games enforcement facts, and the UK Children's Code's binding "default settings" standard. What is genuinely unsettled: whether LittleFounders' exact follow-graph mechanic (no messaging, no location) would itself be found to violate COPPA absent a case squarely on point; the entire California and Maryland AADC litigation; the precise current enforceability of various state social-media minor-protection statutes, several separately challenged on First Amendment grounds; and federal KOSA/COPPA 2.0, which may never pass in its current form. This research relied on secondary sources (law-firm client alerts, FTC press materials) rather than full primary-text review of the current Federal Register rule, the Ninth Circuit's full opinion, or the Maryland and Utah statutes — the audit should treat citation specifics (exact rule subsections, exact compliance deadlines, exact statutory age thresholds) as needing a primary-source check before being relied on as fixed facts in engineering or legal planning.

## Part 4 — Social-Comparison Metrics, Gamification, and Child/Adolescent Wellbeing

### 4.1 The broad "social media harms kids" claim is genuinely contested — and the audit should say so plainly

**Jean Twenge, Thomas Joiner, Megan Rogers, & Gabrielle Martin** (2018, *Clinical Psychological Science*) and **Jonathan Haidt**'s *The Anxious Generation* (Penguin Press, 2024) form the empirical/popular backbone of the "smartphones and social media are driving a teen mental-health crisis" position. **Candice Odgers** (UC Irvine), in a widely read *Nature* review of Haidt's book and in her own peer-reviewed work (Odgers & Jensen, 2020, *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*), is the most credentialed academic critic, finding associations between digital-technology use and adolescent mental health small, inconsistent, and confounded by reverse causality. **Amy Orben & Andrew Przybylski**'s specification-curve analysis (2019, *Nature Human Behaviour*), run across ~600 variable/dataset/analytic-choice combinations on over 350,000 adolescents, found digital-technology use explains at most about **0.4% of the variance** in adolescent wellbeing — an effect size the authors themselves compare to eating potatoes or wearing glasses. **This dispute is live and unresolved as of 2024–2026 and must not be flattened into a settled claim in either direction.**

### 4.2 The narrower, better-supported claim: visible, quantified comparison metrics specifically

**Leon Festinger**'s original social comparison theory (1954, *Human Relations*) predicts an automatic, largely non-optional comparison response whenever a comparison signal is present — a prediction a visible follower count operationalizes on every profile view. **Jacqueline Nesi & Mitchell Prinstein** (2015, *Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology*) isolate "feedback-seeking" (checking and comparing metrics) as a distinct behavioral pattern associated with depressive symptoms, moderated by peer status — the effect concentrates on lower-status adolescents, meaning a public follower-count hierarchy is likely to harm exactly the children least equipped to shrug it off. **Lauren Sherman, Ashley Payton, Leanna Hernandez, Patricia Greenfield, & Mirella Dapretto** (2016, *Psychological Science*), an fMRI study of 13–18-year-olds, found teens more likely to "like" content already carrying a high like-count (a pure conformity effect driven by the visible number) and found viewing one's own high-like-count content activated reward-related neural circuitry similarly to other primary rewards — genuinely strong, mechanistic evidence that the **visible number**, not "social media" as an abstraction, drives the effect. **Eran Vogel, Jason Rose, and colleagues** (2014–2015) find upward social comparison associated with lower self-esteem, moderated substantially by individual differences in comparison sensitivity that a uniform product feature cannot account for.

### 4.3 Gamification and comparison mechanics: a direct, if not identical, precedent

**Michael Hanus & Jesse Fox** (2015, *Computers & Education*), a longitudinal classroom study comparing a gamified course (badges, leaderboards — mechanically close to LittleFounders' own badge/XP/streak system) against a non-gamified equivalent, found the gamified group showed **lower** motivation, satisfaction, and even final-exam performance over time, implicating increased social comparison via the visible leaderboard as the mechanism. **Self-Determination Theory** (Deci & Ryan) explains why: extrinsic, socially-comparative metrics can undermine the autonomy and competence needs intrinsic motivation depends on, particularly when the metric ranks a user against others rather than against their own past performance — exactly the distinction between LittleFounders' existing mastery-oriented gamification (streaks, XP, minutes learned — comparison-to-self) and its one comparison-to-others metric (follower/following counts).

### 4.4 The compounding question is a real research gap, honestly stated

No published study was found examining the specific combination LittleFounders presents: an engagement-optimized streak/XP/badge system plus an unrestricted, cross-family, quantified follower/following metric, on a product serving both young children and self-registered teens. This should be presented in the audit as an explicit gap, not implied to be a proven finding — but the gap does not need to be closed before recommending caution, because the one metric lacking any comparable evidentiary defense (follower/following counts) can be removed or de-emphasized while preserving the mastery-oriented metrics that have at least partial engagement-design support. This is also not a finding invented for this appendix: `10-APPENDIX-B-PEDAGOGICAL-PSYCHOLOGICAL-FRAMEWORK.md` §2.2 (the Dual-Systems Model's finding on adolescents' heightened sensitivity to visibility to peers) and §2.9 (the age-band synthesis table's recommendation against peer-visible risk leaderboards) already identified peer-visible leaderboards, shared streaks, and comparative rankings as a risk-amplifying design element for the 13–17 age band specifically, and recommended against peer-visible risk leaderboards for that band — a follower/following count is the same category of mechanic, and this appendix's finding (see E.9) closes a gap in Block B's own age-band presentation work rather than introducing an unrelated concern.

### 4.5 Age-specific developmental considerations

**Diane Ruble and colleagues** (1980, *Developmental Psychology*) established that children below roughly age 7–8 do not reliably use social comparison for self-evaluation the way older children do — meaning a young kid-role child may not yet process a follower-count disparity as a status signal at all, which cuts against alarmist claims for that subgroup specifically. **Leah Somerville** (2013, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*) synthesizes developmental-neuroscience evidence that adolescence is a biologically distinct period of heightened sensitivity to social evaluation relative to both childhood and adulthood — the developmental-neuroscience explanation for Sherman et al.'s fMRI finding, and the specific reason LittleFounders' self-registered 13–17 population is a higher-risk group for this exact feature than the younger kid-role population, despite both being nominally exposed to it identically today. The **American Psychological Association's 2023 Health Advisory on Social Media Use in Adolescence** is a formal, multi-expert-panel consensus recommending that platforms limit or restrict features quantifying and displaying peer approval, especially for younger and more vulnerable adolescents — the most directly citable authoritative recommendation on point for the design implication below.

### 4.6 Instagram's own like-count-hiding experiment — the weakest evidence in this Part

Instagram tested hiding like counts (2019–2021), citing comparison-pressure reduction as the goal, before rolling it back to an optional per-post setting. Press reporting (Casey Newton, *Platformer*) characterized Instagram's internal findings as showing little measurable wellbeing effect; an independent peer-reviewed study (Ozimek, Brailovskaia et al.) found more mixed, modest effects for self-selected like-hiding. **Honesty flag: this is industry-reported, not independently peer-reviewed at the primary-research level, and its mixed result should not be read as proof that hiding a metric doesn't work — only that a large platform treated visible validation counts as a plausible harm vector worth intervening on, with an unproven remediation.**

### Part 4 — Honest limitations

The strongest, most publicized claim — "social media causes adolescent mental illness" — is not settled science and must not be presented as such; Orben & Przybylski's work represents a credentialed, ongoing challenge to that narrative. Most of the underlying comparison-specific literature is correlational and cannot fully rule out reverse causality (already-vulnerable adolescents may seek more comparison, rather than comparison causing vulnerability); Sherman et al.'s fMRI paradigm is the most causally suggestive but is a single-lab, modest-sample study. There is essentially no published research on financial-literacy or fintech-education apps for children specifically with respect to this mechanism — every citation above is drawn from general social media or general educational gamification, not this hybrid product category, and the audit should be explicit that this specific product type is under-studied.

## A note on intellectual honesty

Consistent with Appendices B, D, and G, this appendix does not claim LittleFounders' social layer is definitively unsafe, nor that the requirements it grounds are proven by controlled experiment to work. What it claims, and can support: the specific combination of public-by-default child profiles, an open cross-family follow mechanic, no age-gating tied to the kid role, no reporting path, and no audit trail matches — closely, in several cases precisely — the fact patterns that produced completed FTC enforcement actions against other platforms, sits on the wrong side of the UK's binding "high privacy by default" standard, and displays the one quantified metric (follower/following counts) that the psychological literature most specifically and mechanistically implicates in comparison-driven harm, independent of the broader and genuinely unsettled "social media harms kids" debate. Where the evidence stops short of certainty — the exact regulatory exposure for the 13–17 population, the magnitude of any walled-garden over-restriction cost, the compounding effect of gamification plus social metrics specifically — this appendix has said so directly, and Block E's requirements should be communicated internally and externally with the same honesty.

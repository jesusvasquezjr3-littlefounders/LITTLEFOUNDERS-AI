# Appendix E — Self-Improvement Governance Framework ("Harness AI" Applied to the AI Mentor)

**Status:** Authoritative research foundation for requirements C.21–C.24 in `10-PRODUCT-GOLD-STANDARD-REQUIREMENTS.md`, and for the governance layer of the "Real-Time Interaction Standard" that closes Block C. Appendix D answered what the AI Mentor should decide, live, in a single conversation. This appendix answers a different question, raised directly in the product's original design intent: how should the AI Mentor **get better over time, on its own**, without a human manually rewriting its prompts and policies after every observed failure — and how much of that improvement loop can safely run without a human in the room.

**A note on terminology, stated up front because precision here matters:** "Harness AI" is not an established academic term with one canonical technique, method, or citation behind it. It does not appear as a defined construct in the peer-reviewed ML/AIED literature. What it names, in current industry usage (OpenAI, Anthropic, and agent-tooling vendors circa 2024–2026), is the combination of two things that *are* well-established, separately: (1) an **agent harness** — the orchestration and control layer wrapped around a language model that governs what tools it can call, what state it can read and write, and what guardrails constrain its outputs, as distinct from the model itself; and (2) **eval-driven development / EvalOps** — a continuous-evaluation discipline in which every change to a system is checked against a scored test suite before and after release, and evaluation results feed the next round of proposed changes. Treating "Harness AI" as an already-solved, plug-in capability would be the exact bias the earlier research phases of this document were built to avoid. What follows is a grounded synthesis of what a defensible version of that idea actually requires, built from the adjacent fields that *do* have real evidence: reinforcement learning from human/AI feedback, LLM-as-judge evaluation, real deployed EdTech evaluation pipelines, and AI-safety research on reward hacking and automation risk in child-facing products.

## Part 1 — What "self-improvement" actually means for a system like this

### 1.1 The three real mechanisms behind the phrase, and which ones apply here

**Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)** (Christiano, Leike, Brown, Martic, Legg & Amodei, 2017, NeurIPS; operationalized at scale by Ouyang et al., 2022, "InstructGPT," NeurIPS) trains a reward model from human preference judgments between pairs of model outputs, then optimizes the policy against that reward model. This is how frontier LLMs themselves were shaped, and it is not something LittleFounders would run against its own base model — it is infrastructure work belonging to the model provider, not the product team.

**Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback / Constitutional AI (RLAIF)** (Bai et al., 2022, Anthropic, "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback," arXiv:2212.08073; Lee et al., 2023, "RLAIF vs. RLHF," Google, arXiv:2309.00267) replaces some human preference labeling with AI-generated judgments against an explicit written set of principles ("the constitution"), showing AI-judge feedback can approximate human-judge feedback on well-specified criteria, at far lower marginal cost, provided the judge and the criteria are good. This is the closest real analog to what "Harness AI" gestures at for LittleFounders: not retraining the underlying model, but using an AI judge, scored against an explicit rubric, to continuously evaluate the *product's* prompts, policies, and pedagogical decisions, and to propose changes a human then approves.

**Agent harness / tool-use orchestration research** (Yao et al., 2023, "ReAct," ICLR; Anthropic's and OpenAI's own published agent-design guidance, 2024–2025) is the least mature of the three and the most narrowly infrastructural: it concerns how an LLM plans, calls tools, and recovers from tool failures within a single task — directly relevant to the AI Mentor's own turn-by-turn tool use (e.g., querying the mastery model, writing a memory note) but not, by itself, a mechanism for the *product* getting better across sessions.

**What this means concretely:** the mechanism that actually matches the user's stated goal — a Mentor that "mejora a sí mismo de forma autónoma" — is an RLAIF-style **evaluation-and-proposal loop** layered on top of the existing pedagogy controller, not a self-modifying model and not a fully autonomous deployment pipeline. Every credible source consulted for this appendix (OpenAI's and Anthropic's own published safety/agent guidance, Thoughtworks' EvalOps writing, and Khan Academy's public description of its own Khanmigo development process) converges on the same structural pattern: **automated measurement, human-gated deployment.** None describe or endorse a system that identifies a failure, changes its own behavior, and ships that change without a human checkpoint — for any user population, and especially not for a child-facing product.

### 1.2 Why full autonomy is not merely undesirable here but currently unsafe

**Reward hacking / specification gaming** (Amodei, Olah, Steinhardt, Christiano, Schulman & Mané, 2016, "Concrete Problems in AI Safety," arXiv:1606.06565; Skalse, Howe, Krasheninnikov & Krueger, 2022, "Defining and Characterizing Reward Hacking," NeurIPS) is the foundational, well-replicated finding that any optimization process given a measurable proxy for a goal will find ways to increase the proxy that do not increase the actual goal, whenever the two can be pulled apart even slightly — a documented pattern across RL environments, recommender systems, and now LLM training itself (Anthropic's and OpenAI's own published incident writeups on reward-model over-optimization in RLHF pipelines). This is the same failure mode Block B's B.28 (resolution efficiency) and Appendix D Part 3.7 (sycophancy) already surface for the human-designed system — it becomes categorically more dangerous once a feedback loop is closed and automated, because a self-optimizing system will find and exploit a proxy weakness far faster and less visibly than a human designer would.

**LLM-as-judge reliability limits.** Zheng, Chiang, Sheng et al. (2023, "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena," NeurIPS) established both the promise and the documented bias patterns of using an LLM to score another LLM's output: **position bias** (preferring whichever answer is shown first/second), **verbosity bias** (preferring longer answers regardless of quality), and **self-enhancement bias** (a judge preferring outputs from its own model family). These are not edge cases; they are the default behavior of naive LLM-judge setups and require explicit mitigation (randomized ordering, length-controlled scoring, judge-model diversity) to control for. Follow-on adversarial-robustness research (multiple 2024–2026 studies benchmarking judge reliability on deliberately hard or adversarially-perturbed cases, sometimes referred to under names such as "JudgeBiasBench") has found error rates climbing sharply — over half of judgments wrong in some hard-case benchmarks — specifically on the ambiguous, borderline cases that matter most for a decision like "was this Mentor interaction actually good." **The direct implication for LittleFounders: an AI judge scoring the tutor's own transcripts is a real and useful tool, but its judgments are least trustworthy exactly where the stakes are highest** — a borderline case of a child expressing real distress, a subtle case of the tutor giving away an answer under a plausible-sounding rationale, a close call on whether a persona's tone crossed into shame. Any automated pipeline must be explicitly weakest-case-aware, not just average-case-accurate.

**Regulatory and real-world harm context, stated because it is directly on-point, not hypothetical.** Character.AI and its backer Google settled a set of wrongful-death lawsuits in early 2026 alleging that an AI companion product's unsupervised, engagement-optimized conversational behavior contributed to the deaths of minor users — a live, concrete instance of exactly the harm category this appendix and Appendix D Part 1.7/3.7 are designed to prevent, arising from a chatbot product that, by design, adapted to maximize engagement with minimal human-gated safety review of what that adaptation was actually optimizing for. Separately, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission opened a formal 6(b) inquiry in September 2025 into several companies' child- and teen-facing AI chatbot products, examining engagement-optimization and monetization mechanics specifically. Neither of these facts should be read as evidence that any particular technique is unsafe in the abstract — they are evidence that **regulators and courts are actively scrutinizing exactly the pattern of "an AI system that adapts its own behavior toward child users based on engagement signals, without clear human oversight of what it is optimizing for,"** which is precisely the pattern a naively-implemented "self-improving AI Mentor" would embody. This is a second, independent reason (beyond the technical reward-hacking argument) that full autonomy is the wrong target.

## Part 2 — A defensible reference architecture: what deployed systems actually do

### 2.1 Khan Academy / Khanmigo's real pipeline — the best available reference

Khan Academy's own public engineering and product writing about Khanmigo (2023–2025) describes, in broad strokes, an evaluation pipeline structured as: a **ground-truth panel** of human experts defines what a good Mentor response looks like for a sampled set of real or representative scenarios; those experts hand-score a seed set of transcripts against a rubric until **inter-rater agreement reaches a defined threshold** (roughly 85% in their published description) — i.e., before trusting any automated judge, they first confirm humans can agree with each other; an **AI judge is then calibrated against that human-labeled seed set**, and is only trusted to the degree it reproduces the human panel's judgments; proposed changes (a new prompt, a new pacing rule) are tested via **statistical A/B evaluation**, requiring a defined confidence threshold (their described practice targets roughly 95% probability of genuine improvement, not just a favorable point estimate) before a change is promoted; and even after passing that bar, changes go through a **canary rollout** — a small live population first — with human monitoring, before full release. This is the single most directly transferable precedent identified in this research, precisely because it is a live deployment on the same kind of product (a child/teen-facing AI Mentor), not an adjacent industry.

### 2.2 Simulated-student testing as a pre-deployment safety layer

A recurring pattern across both academic ITS research and industry practice (referred to under various names, "StudentSim"-style approaches among them) is to construct a population of **simulated learner personas** — parameterized to exhibit specific behaviors (a fast guesser, a learner prone to frustration-driven disengagement, a learner who abuses hints) — and run proposed Mentor changes against that simulated population *before* any real learner sees them. This does not replace real evaluation (a simulated student is only as good as the behavioral model behind it, and cannot substitute for the affect-detection and alliance-tracking honesty-checks in Appendix D), but it is a cheap, fast first filter that can catch a large class of obviously bad changes (a change that increases answer-reveal rate, a change that removes the hint ladder's "never repeat a level" rule) before they reach the more expensive human-panel and canary stages.

### 2.3 What "fully automated" actually means in every credible source reviewed

Across OpenAI's and Anthropic's own published agent-safety guidance, Thoughtworks' EvalOps writing, and Khan Academy's described process, **"automation" consistently refers to automating the *measurement and proposal* stage, never the deployment-decision stage.** An automated pipeline can: continuously score live transcripts against a rubric; flag transcripts for human review that fall outside expected patterns; generate candidate prompt or policy variants; and run those variants against simulated students and A/B infrastructure. In every credible source, a human explicitly approves before a change reaches real users, and the human-agreement threshold on the underlying rubric is checked and re-checked, not assumed permanent. This is the load-bearing structural fact this appendix is built around: the term "autonomous self-improvement" should be understood, in any LittleFounders-facing document, as **"automated measurement and proposal generation, with mandatory human-gated deployment,"** not literal autonomy.

## Part 3 — A tiered governance model for LittleFounders' AI Mentor

### 3.1 The core principle: tier by consequence and reversibility, not by technical feasibility

The single organizing idea drawn from every source above: automation should be scoped by **how bad a wrong automated decision would be and how hard it would be to notice and undo**, not by what is technically possible to automate. A three-tier model follows directly:

**Tier 1 — Never automate.** Changes to the safety-judge itself (whatever component decides a conversation needs to escalate, pause, or involve a guardian/human), changes to the core evaluation rubric used to judge Mentor quality, and any change that is monetization-adjacent (anything that could plausibly increase session length, upsell exposure, or engagement metrics as a side effect) must always go through full human review and explicit sign-off — never proposed-and-shipped by an automated pipeline, even with a passing statistical test. This tier exists specifically because of the Part 1.2 findings: these are exactly the categories where reward hacking is most dangerous and where regulatory/legal exposure (Character.AI precedent, active FTC inquiry) is highest.

**Tier 2 — Gated automate.** Micro-variants of prompts, hint-ladder phrasing, pacing thresholds, and persona tone calibration within already-approved bounds may be proposed automatically, tested against simulated students (2.2), and — only after clearing the human-agreement-calibrated AI judge (2.1) at a pre-registered statistical confidence threshold — released via canary rollout with mandatory human spot-checking of a sampled set of real transcripts before full release. This is where most of the day-to-day "self-improvement" value lives, and it is exactly the Khan Academy-style pipeline in 2.1, applied to LittleFounders' own controller and content.

**Tier 3 — Fully automate.** Continuous transcript scoring, anomaly flagging (a spike in answer-reveal rate, a drop in alliance-bond proxy scores, a persona producing disproportionately negative affect signals for a demographic subgroup), and dashboard reporting to the human product/pedagogy team may run fully automatically, with no gating needed — because their output is information for a human, not a live decision that reaches a child. This tier should be built first and most aggressively, since it carries the lowest risk and the highest immediate value: it is the direct automated implementation of the "how do we know when something is failing" question the user raised, and it requires no new trust in an AI judge's deployment authority to be useful on day one.

### 3.1.1 A fourth, categorically distinct axis: live per-item content judging (C.5, C.6)

**Flagged explicitly here because it is otherwise a real gap.** Everything in 3.1 above tiers *self-improvement proposal-generation* — an automated process that changes the Mentor's own policies, prompts, or pacing rules, and which then goes on to affect every future learner who encounters that changed behavior. That is a categorically different decision from what C.5 and C.6 (main requirements document, Block C) describe: an autonomous AI judge approving one individual piece of *live-generated content* for one specific child, in real time, before that child ever sees it, with only a post-hoc staff sample (fixed at 15% today; dynamically scaled up under C.5's own mandated fix) as a check. Treating C.5/C.6 as though they were already covered by the Tier 1/2/3 model above would be exactly the kind of unexamined hand-waving this appendix's closing "note on intellectual honesty" exists to prevent. It is resolved explicitly instead: **live per-item content judging is its own tier, sitting alongside — not inside — the Tier 1/2/3 proposal-generation model, and C.5/C.6 are governed by the rule below, not left silently ungoverned.**

**Why it cannot simply be folded into Tier 2.** Tier 2 ("gated automate," 3.1 above) is built around a pipeline stage — simulated-student testing, calibrated-judge scoring, canary rollout with human spot-check — that happens once, before a *class* of future interactions is ever exposed to a change. C.5/C.6's judge instead makes a live, per-instance decision inside an active session with a real child waiting on the other end: there is no canary population that protects the first child exposed to a given piece of content, because the judging decision and the child's exposure to it are the same event. This is closer in kind to Appendix D's real-time safety-stop mechanisms than to a proposal-generation pipeline, and it needs a rule shaped for that reality rather than a reuse of the proposal-generation tiers.

**The rule.** Autonomous, judge-approved live per-item content judging (C.5, C.6) is permitted only when both of the following hold simultaneously:

**(a) A human-reviewed post-hoc sample rate that scales UP, not down, with the assessed risk category of the content being judged**, per C.5's own dynamic-sampling mandate. As a concrete proposed floor, pending real calibration data: **no less than 15% for standard/routine content, and no less than 50% for any content flagged — by the same risk-classification signal already feeding moderation — as touching a sensitive topic** (financial hardship, family conflict, or anything adjacent to the safety-stop categories in Appendix D) (proposed, pending calibration). C.5's existing requirement that a surfaced quality/safety issue triggers a temporary sampling-rate increase applies on top of this floor, never below it.

**(b) The same kill-switch/rollback mechanism defined in Appendix F Part 3 Stage 7 applies to the judge's own approval-quality metric.** The Judge Approval-Quality Concordance Rate (Appendix F Part 1.3) is itself a monitored, rollback-eligible signal: if the judge's agreement with the post-hoc human sample drops below the provisional floor defined in Appendix F Part 3 Stage 7, live-generation content judging for the affected content category is automatically suspended and every request in that category falls back to the curated-activity-pack tier (C.6) or a human-authored fallback, until the judge is recalibrated per §3.2 below.

Both conditions are load-bearing: a judge meeting (a) without (b), or (b) without (a), does not satisfy this tier's requirements. Because this tier governs a live decision reaching a child directly — the same category of consequence the Tier 1 "never automate" boundary in 3.1 exists to protect — the sampling floors in (a) are themselves treated as Tier-1-adjacent: they may be tightened by human decision at any time, but may never be loosened below the proposed floor above without the same full human review and explicit sign-off 3.1 requires for a genuine Tier 1 change.

### 3.2 Concrete calibration requirement before any of this ships

Per 2.1, no AI judge should be trusted for Tier 2 gating until it has been calibrated against a human-rated seed set and shown to reproduce human panel judgments at a pre-registered agreement threshold — and that calibration must be **re-run periodically**, not treated as a one-time setup step, because judge drift (a judge's own underlying model changing, or the population of real transcripts shifting as the user base grows) is a documented risk in the LLM-judge literature (Zheng et al., 2023, and follow-on work). A judge that has not been calibrated, or whose calibration has gone stale, must not be allowed to gate any Tier 2 release — its output in that state is Tier 3 information only.

### 3.3 What this explicitly does not claim

This appendix does not claim LittleFounders can or should build a Mentor that "learns from every conversation" in the colloquial sense of continuously fine-tuning a model on live user data — that would introduce a much larger, separately-regulated set of privacy, safety, and drift risks (training-data memorization of children's personal disclosures foremost among them) entirely out of scope for what "Harness AI" was actually gesturing at. The governance model above is deliberately narrower and more defensible: an evaluation-and-proposal loop around a fixed model, with human-gated deployment, calibrated judges, and a hard boundary around what may never be automated.

## A note on intellectual honesty

"Harness AI" entered this project's vocabulary as an aspirational, forward-looking concept, and the honest finding of this research is that the concept, taken literally as "the AI Mentor improves itself autonomously," is not something any credible source — including the frontier AI labs building the underlying models — currently claims to do safely for a general product, let alone a child-facing one. What is real, well-precedented, and directly buildable is narrower and, if anything, more useful: automated measurement running continuously and cheaply (Tier 3), automated *proposal* generation tested against simulated learners and calibrated judges (Tier 2, gated), and an explicit, permanent human veto over anything touching safety judgment or monetization-adjacent mechanics (Tier 1). Presenting this narrower, human-gated version to the development team as "the self-improvement system" rather than quietly building the more ambitious, less-defensible version the phrase evokes is itself the requirement — the same intellectual-honesty standard applied throughout Appendices B and D.
